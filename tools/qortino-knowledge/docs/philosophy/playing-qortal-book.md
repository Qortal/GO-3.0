## 1.1 It is all my fault

"You always get what You want." ~ Alan Watts Have You ever wondered why a few people may "legally" print money, while anyone else doing the very same thing is imprisoned for it?

I say my fault on purpose. Not to sink into guilt, but because what we made together, we can also unmake.
We were born into a system built on a single belief: that money has value. The money we use is called fiat money.
In Latin, fiat means "let it be done on faith", worth summoned by decree, the way a ruler's word once made a thing so. It has value only for as long as we agree it does.
The instant that faith wavers, the money wavers with it.
Every crisis, small or large, is simply that faith flickering.

And with each flicker, the value quietly saved by ordinary people is drained away.
So how is the money actually made?
Most of us picture a bank as a vault, it gathers the savings of people and lends them to others. That is not what happens. When a bank grants You a loan, it moves no one else's money to You. It simply writes a number into Your account. That number did not exist a moment before. It was created, out of nothing, by typing it in. You need not take my word for this: the Bank of England described it plainly in 2014: every time a bank makes a loan, it creates brand‑new money in the very same act.
The catch is the interest. The bank creates the sum You borrowed, the principal. It does not create the interest You owe on top of it. So across the whole world, more is always owed than was ever made. The only way for You to find the difference is to take it from someone else, someone who borrowed before You, or who will borrow after. Everyone can never repay at once. It was never built to be possible.
Money is debt.
This is how the largest pyramid in the world is raised, not in stone, but in numbers, with all of us living inside it. For the accounts to balance, someone, somewhere, must always fall: into bankruptcy, into foreclosure, sometimes into a despair they do not come back from. That is not a flaw in the system. It is the design. A chair is taken away each round, and the music keeps playing. You already know this game. We call it musical chairs.

The Game of Musical Chairs Ten children circle nine chairs while the music plays.
When it stops, they scramble for a seat. First come, first served, and because there are ten of them and only nine chairs, one child is always left standing. Not because that child was too slow, or careless, or unworthy. Because the game was built with one chair too few. Someone has to lose. That was decided before the music ever started.
This is how almost all money is made. Nearly every coin begins as a loan. The lender creates the money it lends, but it never creates the interest. So from the very first day, more is owed than was ever made. The chairs are the money. The children are all of us. And the missing chair is the interest: a debt the system demands but never prints.
Then the next round begins, and a chair is taken away. The circle tightens. The music stops. Another child is out. Then another, and another, a chair vanishing each time, when someone is falling.
And these are not really children, and this is not really a game. The one left standing loses a home, a business, a family farm held for three generations. Sometimes the debt was never even theirs, quietly inherited from a relative they barely knew. The judge does not care that they never agreed to play. Behind every empty chair is a real life. Some are pushed to despair, and not all of them come back.
On it goes, chair after chair, round after round, until one sits alone among empty seats and is named the winner, while nine stand in the dark and are named losers.

"You went to a place, most days, for most of the hours You were awake, and You did what You were told there, and at the end they gave You some."
"What place?"
"Any place. Often a place You hated."

She stopped pressing the soil. "You spent the hours of Your life in a place You hated?"
"Most people did. Years of it. Whole lives of it." He smiled at her face. "We even had a machine beside the bed that screamed at us before the sun came up, so we wouldn't be late to it."
She stared at him as though he had told her people used to walk on their hands. "Why would anyone build a machine to do that?"
"So we could get the numbers," he said gently. "And You needed the numbers even to sleep indoors. You paid them, every month, just to have a roof. If You stopped paying, they put You out under the sky."
"Whose sky?"
"That was rather the question," he said, "that nobody thought to ask."
He told her the rest while the light climbed the hill. He told her about food - mountains of it, thrown away to rot behind the shops, while on the very same street people went to bed hungry, because hunger and plenty had nothing to do with each other; only with the numbers. He told her about the locks. That every door had one, and every window, and that people lay awake afraid of one another, and taught their children to fear the stranger on the path.
That was the one she could not hold.
"Afraid of me?" she said. "If I'd walked up like this?"
"They'd have wondered what You wanted to take."

She looked down at the coin in her hand, and then at him, and he watched her try, honestly, with her whole open heart, to imagine it, a world where a girl helping an old man plant a tree was something to be feared, and he watched her fail. She simply could not get there. It was too strange. And the old man had to look away at the valley for a moment, because there is a particular ache in watching someone be unable to imagine the thing that nearly broke You.
"Why," she said at last - and it was the question he had been waiting fifty years to be asked by someone who truly didn't know the answer - "why didn't they just stop?"
"Someone did," he said. "In the end. That's the whole story, really."
He told her how it began - not with armies, not with banners, nothing You could photograph. Just a strange little game, at first, that a few odd people played on their computers. It kept a record of Your time, and gave You a small thing in return they'd called it QORT - that was yours, that no king or company could print or take. And the game had one rule that everyone laughed at: don't sell it. Don't trade Your time back for their numbers.
"And the machines were already coming, by then," he said.
"Year by year they took up the dull work, and the heavy work, and the dangerous work that no one ever wanted, until, slowly, a person's hours stopped belonging to the men with the numbers, and became their own again, to keep or to give." He turned a clod of soil over in his fingers. "But for the longest while, almost no one could hear it. They only wanted to know what the QORT was worth. In numbers. They couldn't understand that that was the wrong question." He shook his

head. "Then, here and there, one person stopped selling. Then a family. Then a whole village decided, together - we have enough, we have each other, we will never put a price on an hour of a human life again. And the strangest thing happened.
Nothing fell apart. The bread still got baked. The children still got taught - taught who they really were, this time. And the people in the next valley looked over and thought: well. If they can…"
"And then everyone stopped," she said.
"Not everyone, not at once. It took most of a lifetime. My lifetime." He was quiet a moment. "I was one of the early ones, You see. Back when it was still foolish."
"Was it hard?"
"It was lonely," he said simply, and did not soften it for her.
"People I loved thought I'd lost my mind. I missed years of meals at certain tables. There were nights I lay awake under a roof I was still paying for, and was certain I had wasted my one life on an imaginary game." He pressed the last of the earth down around the little oak. "I planted things, in those years, I knew I'd never sit beneath. You do it anyway. You do it for the someone who'll sit there. For You, as it turns out."
She was quiet for a long time. Then she held the coin back out to him.
"No," he said. "Keep it. So You remember it was real. So You don't think I made it up." He closed her hand around it again.
"Thank You for helping an old man dig a hole."
And she said the thing they all said, easy as breathing, without a thought in the world that it was anything at all:

"Sure."
And she reached into her bag and pressed a plum into his hand, warm from the road, and squeezed his knotted fingers, and that was when the old man began, very quietly, to cry.
Because that was it. That was the whole of it. Right there in his palm, a plum, given to a stranger for nothing, by a girl who did not know she was paying him in the only currency that had ever been real, who could not even conceive of the other kind, who had to be told, like a fairy story, that her own kind had once locked their doors against each other.
We had won. He had lived to see that we had won.
"Why are You crying?" she asked, alarmed, kneeling closer.
"Are You hurt?"
He laughed, and wiped his face with the back of a dirty hand, and shook his head. "No, no. These are good tears. The best kind." He patted the soil beside the sapling. "Listen. This is my tree. One day soon - not long now - they'll lay me back into the ground right here, and I'll go up into it, leaf by leaf. They'll put a little tablet there with my name, and a line about my life." He looked at her, his eyes bright and wet and entirely unafraid.
"Will You come back, someday, and tell it a funny story? It likes those. So do I."
"I will," she said, and he saw that she meant it.
She stood, and dusted the earth from her knees, and walked on up the path into the morning, and the old man stayed there a while longer on his knees beside the small oak, in no hurry at all, eating his plum in the sun.

It all happens on-chain, inside the Q-Mintership app, overseen by the community-elected Minter Admins. What is the Integral Collective Federation (ICF)?

People sometimes ask whether QORT is the same as Integral's Time Credits, the ITC. Not quite, and the difference matters. The moment You decide never to sell
