# gardening — golden FAQ

## Q: How do I improve garden soil?

A: Feed the soil, not just the plant: add 3–5 cm of compost to beds every year, keep soil covered with mulch or living plants, and disturb it as little as possible. Organic matter feeds soil life, holds water in sand, opens clay, and buffers pH. Stick with this yearly habit before chasing specialty amendments.

Also asked as: improve soil · make soil better · build garden soil

## Q: What is the ideal soil pH for most vegetables?

A: Most vegetables thrive around pH 6.0–7.0. Use a cheap pH kit to check; if soil is acidic, add lime slowly over seasons, and if alkaline, add sulfur or lots of organic matter gradually. Avoid big one-time corrections that shock soil life.

Also asked as: vegetable soil pH · soil acidity vegetables · best pH for veggies

## Q: How do I tell if my soil is sand, silt, or clay?

A: Do a jar test: shake a soil sample in water and let it settle into layers—sand sinks first, then silt, then clay on top. Knowing the balance tells you whether you need more organic matter for water-holding (sand) or drainage (clay). Compost helps both extremes.

Also asked as: jar test soil · soil texture test · sand silt clay test

## Q: Why are my plants yellowing from the bottom up?

A: Yellowing that starts on older lower leaves often signals nitrogen hunger. Add compost, well-rotted manure, or a gentle organic nitrogen source such as manure tea, then mulch to keep fertility cycling. Also rule out waterlogging, which can mimic nutrient stress.

Also asked as: yellow lower leaves · nitrogen deficiency garden · plants yellow from bottom

## Q: Should I dig and turn my garden beds every year?

A: Minimize digging. Disturbed soil loses structure and soil life; instead top-dress with compost and keep beds covered. Dig only when establishing new beds, removing deep weeds, or fixing severe compaction.

Also asked as: till garden every year · no dig gardening · should I dig beds

## Q: How wide should vegetable beds be?

A: Make beds about 1–1.2 m wide so you can reach the center from both sides without stepping on soil. Permanent paths concentrate fertility in the beds and prevent compaction. Narrower beds work in tight spaces; just keep walking space firm and separate.

Also asked as: bed width garden · how wide raised beds · garden bed dimensions

## Q: How much sun do vegetables need?

A: Fruiting crops like tomatoes, squash, and peppers want 6–8+ hours of sun. Leafy greens and many roots accept 4–6 hours. Watch your site through a full day before digging so you plant sun-lovers where light actually lands.

Also asked as: sun hours vegetables · how much sunlight garden · shade tolerant vegetables

## Q: Where should I put my vegetable garden?

A: Choose a sunny spot close to the house and a water source—the garden you walk past daily gets tended. Avoid low frost pockets and root competition from large trees when you can. Convenience beats a distant “perfect” site you rarely visit.

Also asked as: best place for garden · where to put vegetable garden · garden location tips

## Q: How do I prepare a new garden bed?

A: Clear weeds, loosen compacted soil if needed, then mix in generous compost with topsoil, especially in raised beds. Shape beds 1–1.2 m wide with permanent paths, water well, and mulch bare soil. Plant after the bed settles and soil can be worked without smearing.

Also asked as: prepare new garden bed · start a vegetable bed · make a new bed

## Q: What does mulch do for soil?

A: Mulch cuts watering needs, suppresses weeds, moderates soil temperature, and feeds soil life as it breaks down. Use straw, leaves, or grass clippings on beds; wood chips suit paths. Keep mulch a little off plant stems to avoid collar rot.

Also asked as: benefits of mulch · why mulch garden · what mulch does

## Q: How do I make compost?

A: Mix roughly 2–3 parts browns (dry leaves, straw, cardboard, wood shavings) to 1 part greens (kitchen scraps, fresh grass, herbivore manure). Keep the pile as moist as a wrung sponge and turn occasionally. Hot piles finish in months; cold piles take about a year.

Also asked as: make compost · compost recipe · how to compost

## Q: What should not go in a compost pile?

A: Avoid meat, dairy, oils, diseased plants, and dog or cat waste—they attract pests or spread pathogens. Stick to plant scraps, leaves, untreated cardboard, and herbivore manure. When in doubt, leave questionable material out of the home pile.

Also asked as: what not to compost · compost don'ts · bad compost materials

## Q: What are compost browns and greens?

A: Browns are carbon-rich dry materials like leaves, straw, cardboard, and wood shavings. Greens are nitrogen-rich wet materials like kitchen scraps, fresh grass, and manure. A rough 2–3:1 brown-to-green ratio keeps compost active without becoming slimy or smelly.

Also asked as: compost browns greens · carbon nitrogen compost · browns vs greens

## Q: Can I compost diseased plants?

A: Generally no for home piles—many disease organisms survive unless the pile runs consistently hot. Bin diseased material away from the garden instead of composting it. Prevention and rotation matter more than trying to sanitize sick plants in a backyard heap.

Also asked as: compost diseased plants · sick plants in compost · plant disease compost

## Q: How much compost should I add each year?

A: A practical universal rate is 3–5 cm of compost spread on beds annually. That maintains organic matter without burying plants. Top-dress in spring or autumn and keep mulch on top.

Also asked as: how much compost · compost application rate · yearly compost amount

## Q: How do I plan a vegetable garden layout?

A: Put tall or trellised crops where they will not shade sun-lovers all day, keep beds 1–1.2 m wide with firm paths, and place the garden near water. Group by family for rotation and leave succession space for repeat sowings. Sketch sun patterns before you dig.

Also asked as: garden layout plan · plan vegetable garden · design garden beds

## Q: What is crop rotation and why do it?

A: Rotate plant families between beds year to year—nightshades, brassicas, legumes, alliums, cucurbits—so soil diseases and pests lose their preferred hosts. Legumes also leave nitrogen behind for the next crop. Even a simple three- or four-bed rotation beats planting tomatoes in the same spot forever.

Also asked as: crop rotation · rotate garden crops · why rotate vegetables

## Q: What is succession sowing?

A: Succession sowing means planting short rows of fast crops every 2–3 weeks instead of one big planting. Lettuce, radish, and beans are classic succession crops that avoid a single glut then nothing. Keep a small seed stash ready for each window.

Also asked as: succession planting · succession sowing · plant every few weeks

## Q: How far apart should I plant vegetables?

A: Follow packet spacing—crowded plants usually yield less than fewer well-spaced ones. Airflow between plants also reduces disease. When in doubt, give fruiting crops more room than leafy greens.

Also asked as: plant spacing vegetables · how far apart plants · crowded vegetable plants

## Q: Which plant families should I rotate?

A: Track nightshades (tomato, pepper, potato, eggplant), brassicas (cabbage, broccoli, kale), legumes (beans, peas), alliums (onion, garlic, leek), and cucurbits (squash, cucumber, melon). Move each family to a different bed next year. Write the map down; memory fails by spring.

Also asked as: vegetable plant families · rotation families garden · nightshade brassica rotation

## Q: How big a garden for winter vegetables?

A: A modest 50 m² managed well can supply a meaningful share of a household’s winter vegetables if you focus on storage crops. Prioritize potatoes, winter squash, dry beans, cabbage, roots, onions, garlic, and kale over salad alone. Intensive beds beat a large neglected plot.

Also asked as: garden size self sufficiency · 50 square metre garden · how big survival garden

## Q: What should I grow for calories in a survival garden?

A: Build the backbone with potatoes, winter squash, dry beans, cabbage, root vegetables, onions, garlic, and kale. Salad greens and herbs add vitamins and morale but will not feed you through winter alone. In cold climates these keepers matter more than summer fruiting crops.

Also asked as: calorie crops garden · survival garden crops · storage crops to grow

## Q: When should I plant garlic?

A: Plant garlic in autumn so cloves root before hard freeze, then harvest the following summer when tops fall and cure until necks are papery. Choose a sunny, well-drained bed and mulch for winter. Spring planting is possible but usually yields smaller bulbs.

Also asked as: plant garlic · when plant garlic · garlic planting time

## Q: How do I grow potatoes?

A: Plant certified or saved seed potatoes in spring after soil is workable, then hill soil or mulch up the stems as they grow. Harvest after tops die back and cure 1–2 weeks in a dark, airy place for storage skins. Potatoes give top calories per square metre for most gardeners.

Also asked as: grow potatoes · planting potatoes · how to hill potatoes

## Q: How do I use a garden calendar?

A: Count backward from your last spring frost and forward from first autumn frost using packet “days to maturity” and “start indoors X weeks” guidance. Note succession sowings every 2–3 weeks and autumn garlic. Write frost dates once; reuse the calendar yearly with variety notes.

Also asked as: garden planting calendar · frost date calendar · when to plant schedule

## Q: How do I garden in a cold climate with a short summer?

A: Choose early varieties by days-to-maturity, start transplants indoors, warm soil with dark mulch or fabric, and use protected microclimates against south-facing walls. Season extenders—row cover, cold frames, low tunnels—buy roughly one climate zone of protection each. Favor potatoes, roots, brassicas, and kale as reliable keepers.

Also asked as: cold climate gardening · short season garden · northern vegetable garden

## Q: What is season extension?

A: Season extension uses covers to grow earlier in spring and later into autumn: row cover on hoops, cold frames, low tunnels, and unheated polytunnels. Each layer of protection roughly buys one climate zone. Start with row cover—it also blocks many insect pests.

Also asked as: extend growing season · season extension methods · grow longer season

## Q: Which vegetables overwinter well?

A: Cold-hardy champions include spinach, mâche, kale, leeks, and chard for autumn and overwintering under modest protection. Parsnips sweeten after frost and store in ground in many climates. Mulch and row cover improve survival through hard freezes.

Also asked as: overwinter vegetables · winter hardy greens · vegetables through winter

## Q: What does days to maturity mean on a seed packet?

A: Days to maturity estimates time from sowing or transplanting to harvest under typical conditions. In short seasons, choose lower numbers and start slow crops indoors. Track your own dates—local weather often shifts the packet estimate.

Also asked as: days to maturity · seed packet maturity · what does DTM mean

## Q: How do I find my last frost date?

A: Use local climate records or long-time gardeners nearby for average last spring frost, then treat it as a planning midpoint, not a guarantee. Count seed-starting weeks backward from that date. Keep row cover ready for late surprises.

Also asked as: last frost date · when is last frost · average frost date

## Q: How does row cover help in cold weather?

A: Lightweight row cover over hoops traps a little heat, blocks wind, and can shield crops from light frost while also excluding many insects. Anchor edges firmly. Remove or vent during unexpected heat so plants do not overheat.

Also asked as: row cover frost · floating row cover · garden fabric cold

## Q: What crops suit short northern summers?

A: Choose early potato, cabbage, kale, root crops, peas, beans, and quick greens; start tomatoes and peppers indoors early. Favor days-to-maturity numbers that fit your frost-free window with margin. Storage crops matter more than long-season melons.

Also asked as: short summer crops · northern garden vegetables · fast maturing vegetables

## Q: When do I start counting seed starting weeks?

A: Count backward from your average last frost date using the packet’s “start indoors X weeks before last frost.” Typical ranges: tomatoes and peppers 6–8 weeks, brassicas 4–6, cucumbers and squash only 2–4. Write the start date on your calendar so seedlings are ready, not root-bound.

Also asked as: weeks before frost seeds · seed starting calendar · count back from frost

## Q: Can kale survive hard frost?

A: Yes—kale often survives hard frost and keeps producing, making it a cold-climate staple. Flavor often improves after cold nights. Protect with mulch or row cover in extreme cold for continuous harvest.

Also asked as: kale frost hardy · does kale survive freeze · winter kale

## Q: Why start vegetables from seed?

A: Seeds cost a fraction of nursery transplants, offer many more varieties, and let you start the season weeks early. Combined with saved seed, seed-starting skill is food security you can store in a jar. Learn on easy crops before fussy ones.

Also asked as: start from seed · why grow from seed · seed starting benefits

## Q: Which crops are easy to start from seed?

A: Forgiving starters include lettuce, spinach, basil, tomato, pepper, cucumber, broccoli, dill, and cilantro; easy flowers include sunflower, calendula, marigold, nasturtium, and zinnia. Master these before specialty crops. Direct-sow beans, peas, and most roots outdoors.

Also asked as: easy seeds to start · beginner seed starting · best first crops from seed

## Q: Which seeds should I direct sow outdoors?

A: Beans, peas, root vegetables (carrot, beet, radish, parsnip), and squash in mild climates often resent transplanting—packets say “direct sow.” Sow in place at the right frost timing. Transplant only if you must, and disturb roots as little as possible.

Also asked as: direct sow seeds · don't transplant vegetables · sow outdoors only

## Q: What soil mix should I use for starting seeds indoors?

A: Use a fine, sterile seed-starting mix—never garden soil or used potting soil indoors, which bring weeds, fungus gnats, and damping-off. Pre-moisten to wrung-out sponge before filling cells. Garden soil belongs outside.

Also asked as: seed starting mix · soil for seedlings · sterile seed mix

## Q: How much light do seedlings need?

A: A south window is often marginal; LED grow lights hung 5–10 cm above seedlings for 14–16 hours daily produce stocky plants. Give them a dark rest at night and raise lights as plants grow. Leggy pale seedlings almost always need more light, closer.

Also asked as: seedling light needs · grow lights seedlings · how much light seedlings

## Q: What temperature do seeds need to germinate?

A: Most seeds germinate fastest at about 18–24 °C soil temperature; peppers and tomatoes like the warm end—use a heat mat if needed. After germination, slightly cooler air grows sturdier plants. Cold, wet mix often rots seed instead of sprouting it.

Also asked as: germination temperature · soil temp for seeds · heat mat seedlings

## Q: How deep should I sow seeds?

A: Sow about 2–3 times the seed’s diameter deep. Tiny seeds and light-requiring seeds such as lettuce are pressed onto the surface. Sow two seeds per cell for insurance, then thin to the strongest.

Also asked as: seed sowing depth · how deep plant seeds · sow seed depth rule

## Q: Should I cover seed trays with plastic?

A: Yes—use a clear dome or wrap for humidity until sprouts appear, then remove it immediately. Trapped humidity after germination invites disease. After uncovering, water from below and add airflow.

Also asked as: humidity dome seedlings · plastic cover seed tray · remove dome after germination

## Q: How should I water seedlings?

A: Water from below: set cells in a tray, pour water into the tray, and let the mix wick moisture up. Let the surface dry slightly between waterings. Bottom watering avoids washing seeds and keeps leaves drier against damping-off.

Also asked as: water seedlings · bottom water trays · how to water seed starting

## Q: When and how do I thin seedlings?

A: Once true leaves appear, snip the weaker seedling at soil level—do not pull, or you may disturb the keeper’s roots. Crowded seedlings all lose. One strong plant per cell beats two weak ones.

Also asked as: thin seedlings · how to thin sprouts · snip weak seedling

## Q: Why did my seeds not germinate?

A: Common causes: soil too cold and wet (seed rots), too dry, sown too deep, or seed too old. Dig one seed up—if soft and swollen, it likely rotted. Retest germination and correct temperature, moisture, and depth.

Also asked as: seeds not germinating · no sprouts seeds · germination failure

## Q: What is damping-off?

A: Damping-off is a fungal collapse where seedlings topple at the base overnight; there is no cure once it strikes. Prevent it with sterile mix, clean containers, bottom watering, and airflow. Remove affected cells so it does not spread.

Also asked as: damping off seedlings · seedlings fall over · seedling stem rot

## Q: Why are my seedlings leggy and pale?

A: Insufficient light—sometimes plus too much warmth—stretches seedlings. Move lights to 5–10 cm above the canopy, give 14–16 hours of light, and cool the room slightly after germination. Leggy plants struggle after transplant.

Also asked as: leggy seedlings · stretched seedlings · pale tall seedlings

## Q: How many weeks before frost do I start tomatoes and peppers?

A: Typically start tomatoes and peppers indoors 6–8 weeks before your last frost date. They need warmth to germinate and strong light afterward. Avoid starting much earlier or they become root-bound and stressed.

Also asked as: start tomatoes indoors · pepper seed starting weeks · tomato weeks before frost

## Q: Can I use garden soil in seed trays?

A: No—garden soil indoors brings weeds, pests, and damping-off fungi. Use sterile seed-starting mix, pre-moistened. Save garden soil for outdoor beds with compost.

Also asked as: garden soil seed trays · use outdoor soil seedlings · potting garden dirt indoors

## Q: What is hardening off seedlings?

A: Hardening off gradually introduces indoor seedlings to outdoor sun, wind, and temperature over 7–10 days. Start in sheltered part shade for a few hours and increase exposure daily, bringing them in or covering at night at first. Skipping this scorches and flattens plants.

Also asked as: harden off plants · hardening off seedlings · acclimate seedlings outdoors

## Q: How do I transplant seedlings into the garden?

A: Transplant on a calm, overcast day or evening after hardening off. Water plants in well, disturb roots gently, and protect from wind the first days. Set at the same depth as in the pot, except tomatoes which can be planted deeper along the stem.

Also asked as: transplant seedlings · plant out seedlings · how to transplant garden

## Q: What happens if I skip hardening off?

A: Indoor seedlings placed straight into full sun and wind get leaf scorch, wilt, and wind damage. Even healthy plants can stall for a week or die. Always budget 7–10 days of gradual exposure.

Also asked as: skip hardening off · transplant shock sunburn · seedlings burned outdoors

## Q: How should I water the garden?

A: Water deep and infrequently: soak the top 15–20 cm root zone, then let the surface dry so roots grow downward. Water soil, not leaves, and prefer morning. Mulch cuts water needs dramatically.

Also asked as: how to water garden · deep watering vegetables · watering wisdom garden

## Q: Is daily shallow watering bad?

A: Yes—shallow daily sprinkles train roots to stay near the surface and leave plants less drought-resilient. Soak thoroughly, then wait until the root zone needs it again. Check moisture 10–15 cm down with a finger or trowel.

Also asked as: shallow watering · water every day bad · daily light watering

## Q: When is the best time to water plants?

A: Morning is best: foliage dries sooner, reducing disease, and plants face the day hydrated. Avoid soaking leaves at night when possible. Drip or soil-level watering is better than overhead spray.

Also asked as: best time water garden · water in morning · evening watering plants

## Q: Should I water leaves or soil?

A: Water the soil at the root zone, not the leaves. Wet foliage encourages disease, while soil soaking builds deep roots. Use drip lines, a watering wand at the base, or ollas.

Also asked as: water soil not leaves · overhead watering disease · root zone watering

## Q: How does mulch reduce watering?

A: Mulch shades soil, slows evaporation, and suppresses thirsty weeds. Straw, leaves, and grass clippings are common bed mulches. After mulching, you will water less often but still soak deeply when you do.

Also asked as: mulch save water · mulch watering needs · reduce irrigation mulch

## Q: How do I know if my garden needs water?

A: Check 10–15 cm down—if soil is dry there, soak the root zone. Wilting in midday heat can be temporary; wilting in the morning usually means real stress. Mulched beds need less frequent checks than bare soil.

Also asked as: when to water garden · check soil moisture · do plants need water

## Q: Can overwatering kill plants?

A: Yes—constant wet soil drowns roots, invites disease, and causes yellowing that looks like nutrient deficiency. Let surfaces dry slightly between deep waterings and improve drainage in compacted beds. Seedlings are especially easy to overwater.

Also asked as: overwatering plants · too much water garden · soggy soil roots

## Q: How do I control garden pests without chemicals?

A: Use an escalation ladder: identify first (many insects are allies), then hand-pick and hose off, use barriers (row cover, cutworm collars, netting), traps (beer for slugs, yellow sticky cards), and only then targeted organics like insecticidal soap, oils, or Bt in the evening. Healthy soil and airflow prevent most problems. Never spray blindly.

Also asked as: organic pest control · pests without chemicals · natural garden pest control

## Q: How do I deal with aphids?

A: Hose aphids off with a strong water spray, encourage ladybugs and other predators, and use insecticidal soap on soft-bodied outbreaks in the evening. Check new growth and leaf undersides often. Avoid excess nitrogen that pushes soft, aphid-friendly growth.

Also asked as: aphid control · get rid of aphids · aphids on plants

## Q: How do I stop slugs organically?

A: Patrol at evening, use boards as day shelters to collect them, try beer traps, copper edges, and iron-phosphate pellets rated for organic use. Reduce wet hiding places and dense ground cover near seedlings. Hand-picking after rain is highly effective.

Also asked as: slug control garden · organic slug killer

## Q: How does row cover prevent insect damage?

A: Row cover is a physical barrier that keeps moths, beetles, and many flying pests off crops while letting light and water through. Install before pests arrive and bury or clip edges sealed. Remove temporarily if crops need insect pollination.

Also asked as: row cover insects · insect barrier fabric · exclude pests row cover

## Q: How do I prevent plant diseases organically?

A: Water soil not foliage, space plants for airflow, remove infected material to the bin (do not compost), and rotate families yearly. Healthy soil reduces stress that invites disease. Clean tools after cutting sick plants.

Also asked as: prevent plant disease · organic disease control · garden fungal disease

## Q: Should I compost diseased leaves?

A: No—bin infected material instead of composting it in a typical home pile. Many pathogens survive and return with the compost. Sanitation plus rotation beats hoping the pile got hot enough.

Also asked as: dispose diseased leaves · don't compost blight · infected plant waste

## Q: Are all insects in the garden bad?

A: No—ladybugs, ground beetles, bees, and many tiny wasps are allies. Identify before you kill or spray. Habitat flowers and avoiding broad sprays protect beneficials that do free pest control.

Also asked as: beneficial insects garden · good bugs garden · identify garden insects

## Q: Why save garden seed?

A: Saved seed means you never start from zero and, year after year, selects plants adapted to your soil and climate. In disruption, viable seed is light, dense, renewable, and tradeable. Pair saving with germination testing each spring.

Also asked as: why save seeds · seed saving importance · home seed bank why

## Q: What is the difference between open-pollinated and hybrid seed?

A: Open-pollinated and heirloom varieties grow true to type from saved seed. F1 hybrids grow from saved seed but split unpredictably into ancestor traits—fine to eat, poor for reliable seed lines. Packets mark hybrids as F1; build your core garden on OP varieties.

Also asked as: open pollinated vs hybrid · F1 hybrid seed saving · heirloom vs hybrid

## Q: Which crops are easiest for seed saving?

A: Self-pollinators are easiest: tomatoes, peas, beans, lettuce, and mostly peppers. Different varieties barely cross, so modest separation or a mesh bag keeps seed pure. Start there before squash, corn, or brassicas.

Also asked as: easy seed saving crops · beginner save seed · self pollinating vegetables

## Q: Which crops cross-pollinate easily?

A: Squash, cucumbers, corn, brassicas, beets/chard, onions, and carrots cross via insects or wind—and related crops can mix (zucchini with pumpkins; carrots with wild carrot). Use distance, timing, or hand-pollination for purity. Grow one variety of a crossing group if you need pure seed.

Also asked as: cross pollinating vegetables · squash cross pollination · keep seed pure

## Q: What are biennial seed crops?

A: Carrots, beets, cabbage, and onions flower in their second year, so roots or plants must survive winter—in ground under mulch or stored and replanted—before yielding seed. Plan ahead or keep purchased seed of biennials as backup. Do not expect seed from first-year roots.

Also asked as: biennial vegetables seed · second year seed crops · onion carrot seed year two

## Q: How do I save dry seeds like beans and lettuce?

A: Let seed mature on the plant until pods rattle or heads brown, finish drying indoors on screens, then thresh and winnow chaff away in a light breeze. Store only snap-dry seed. Label variety and year immediately.

Also asked as: save bean seed · dry process seed saving · thresh winnow seeds

## Q: How do I save tomato seed?

A: Scoop seeds from fully ripe fruit and ferment with pulp and a splash of water 2–3 days until a film forms, then rinse clean and dry on a plate or coffee filter. Fermentation removes germination-inhibiting gel and reduces some diseases. Dry until seeds snap, not bend.

Also asked as: save tomato seeds · ferment tomato seed · tomato seed saving method

## Q: How should I store vegetable seeds?

A: Enemies are moisture, heat, and light. Store bone-dry seed in paper envelopes inside an airtight jar in a cool dark place, with silica gel or a cloth-wrapped spoon of dry rice or powdered milk. Label variety and year; refrigerator storage extends life.

Also asked as: store seeds · seed storage conditions · keep seeds viable

## Q: Can I freeze garden seeds?

A: Yes—truly dry seed stores for many extra years in the freezer. Let jars reach room temperature before opening to avoid condensation on cold seed. Freezing damp seed ruins it.

Also asked as: freeze seeds · freezer seed storage · long term seed bank freeze

## Q: How long do vegetable seeds stay viable?

A: Typical well-stored viability: onions/parsnips 1–2 years; corn, peppers, carrots 2–3; beans, peas, brassicas, squash, tomatoes 3–5+ (tomatoes often longer); lettuce 5–6. Refrigeration roughly doubles these spans. Test doubtful lots each spring.

Also asked as: seed viability years · how long seeds last · seed shelf life

## Q: How do I test seed germination?

A: Roll 10 seeds in a damp paper towel inside a loosely closed bag somewhere warm; check after the packet’s germination window. 8–10 sprouts means sow normally; 5–7 sow thickly; under 5 replace or sow very thickly. Test before the season, not during panic.

Also asked as: germination test seeds · paper towel seed test · test old seeds

## Q: How do I build a household seed bank?

A: Keep three tiers: this year’s working seed, a reserve of last year’s proven varieties, and a deep frozen reserve of core calorie crops enough to replant everything twice. Refresh deep reserves by growing them out every few years. Prioritize OP potatoes (tubers), dry beans, winter squash, kale, cabbage, carrots, onions, and tomatoes.

Also asked as: home seed bank · seed bank tiers · emergency seed supply

## Q: Should I save hybrid seed?

A: You can grow F1 hybrid seed for experiments, but plants will not stay true to the parent. For reliability and trading, save open-pollinated varieties only. Buy hybrids knowingly when you want that specific season’s performance.

Also asked as: save F1 seed · hybrid seed next year · should I save hybrids

## Q: How do I save potato “seed”?

A: Save small healthy tubers for replanting, knowing disease can accumulate over years—refresh with certified seed potatoes when possible. Store like eating potatoes cool and dark, not in the freezer. Potatoes are a top calorie crop worth keeping in the household seed/tuber bank.

Also asked as: save seed potatoes · replant potatoes · potato tuber saving

## Q: What core crops belong in a deep seed reserve?

A: Prioritize open-pollinated dry beans, winter squash, kale, cabbage, carrots, onions, tomatoes, and potato tubers, plus a grain if you have space. Keep enough to replant twice and grow out to refresh. Calorie and storage crops come before novelty varieties.

Also asked as: deep reserve seeds · staple seed list · core seed bank crops

## Q: When should I harvest vegetables?

A: Pick in the cool of morning and handle gently—bruises rot first. Learn crop cues: beans and zucchini small and often; tomatoes at full color; potatoes after tops die back; onions/garlic when tops fall; winter squash when rinds resist a thumbnail. Frequent harvest keeps many plants producing.

Also asked as: when harvest vegetables · best time pick garden · harvest timing

## Q: How do I harvest leafy greens?

A: Use cut-and-come-again: take outer leaves and let the plant keep producing from the center. Morning harvest keeps leaves crisp. Avoid stripping the whole plant unless you want a final cut.

Also asked as: harvest lettuce kale · cut and come again · pick leafy greens

## Q: How do I know potatoes are ready?

A: Harvest after tops die back for storage potatoes, then cure 1–2 weeks in a dark, airy place so skins set. New potatoes can be stolen earlier carefully from the edges. Damaged tubers eat first; store only sound ones.

Also asked as: harvest potatoes · potato ready when · cure potatoes storage

## Q: How do I harvest and cure onions and garlic?

A: Harvest when tops fall, then cure until necks are papery in a dry, airy place. Store cold and dry in mesh bags or braids. Soft or thick-necked bulbs keep poorly—use them first.

Also asked as: cure onions garlic · harvest garlic onions · store cured garlic

## Q: How do I harvest winter squash for storage?

A: Pick when rinds resist a thumbnail, leave stems on, and cure warm for about two weeks. Store cool and dry around 10–15 °C. Cuts and missing stems invite rot in storage.

Also asked as: harvest winter squash · cure squash storage · when pick pumpkin squash

## Q: How do I store vegetables without a root cellar?

A: Match crop needs: potatoes and most roots want cold humid 4–8 °C (basement corner, insulated garage box, buried barrel, or in-ground clamp); onions and garlic cold dry in mesh; winter squash cool dry 10–15 °C; cabbage cold humid. Check every couple of weeks and remove anything softening. Carrots and beets keep well in barely damp sand.

Also asked as: root cellar alternative · store vegetables no cellar · basement vegetable storage

## Q: How do I store carrots and beets?

A: Layer carrots and beets in boxes of barely damp sand in a cold place around 4–8 °C. Trim tops, keep roots undamaged, and check periodically for soft spots. This mimics root-cellar humidity without a dedicated cellar.

Also asked as: store carrots sand · beet storage · keep carrots winter

## Q: What conditions do potatoes need in storage?

A: Store cured potatoes cold and humid, about 4–8 °C, dark and ventilated. One rotten potato spoils neighbors—inspect often. Avoid storing with apples if sprouting becomes a problem in your setup.

Also asked as: potato storage temperature · store potatoes winter · keep potatoes from rotting

## Q: How do I make sauerkraut?

A: Shred cabbage, add 2% salt by weight (20 g per kg), massage until brine flows, pack below brine with a weight, and ferment loosely covered at cool room temperature 1–4 weeks, then cold-store. Cloudy brine and a sour smell are normal; skim white kahm yeast; discard fuzzy or foul growth. The same salt-and-brine method works for many vegetables.

Also asked as: make sauerkraut · ferment cabbage · 2 percent salt ferment

## Q: What is composting?

## Q: How do I fix sandy soil?

## Q: Why do my peppers stay small and few?

A: Peppers need warmth, full sun, and a long enough season—start 6–8 weeks indoors and harden off carefully. Cold soil stunts them; uneven water causes blossom drop. Choose earlier varieties in short summers.

## Q: What should I do when a whole bed fails?

## Q: How deep should I loosen soil for vegetables?

A: Most vegetables use roughly the top 15–30 cm of soil; loosen compaction in that zone when establishing beds, then switch to no-dig top-dressing. Deep watering encourages roots to explore that loosened zone. Paths can stay firm; beds should stay fluffy.

## Q: Should I turn my compost pile?

A: Turning speeds compost by adding air, especially in hot piles. Cold piles still work with little turning—they just finish slower. Turn when the center cools or smells anaerobic, then remoisten if dry.

## Q: Can I use cardboard in compost?

## Q: Should I grow flowers in a vegetable garden?

## Q: How do I winnow seeds?

A: After threshing, pour seed between bowls in a light breeze so chaff blows away while heavier seed falls. Repeat until clean. Work over a sheet so you can recover seed if wind gusts.

## Q: Where should I keep working seed during the season?

A: Keep this year’s working seed cool, dark, and dry in labeled envelopes—kitchen heat and humidity shorten life. A jar in a cupboard beats a shed that bakes. Move long-term reserves to the fridge or freezer.

## Q: Can I dry seeds on a paper towel?

A: Avoid paper towels for wet-processed seed—they glue themselves down. Use a plate or coffee filter instead, then package only when snap-dry. Good drying prevents storage mold.

## Q: Why do my beans get powdery leaves or spots?

A: Leaf diseases increase with wet foliage, crowding, and reused bean ground. Water soil not leaves, space for airflow, rotate legumes, and bin badly infected debris. Healthy soil and morning watering reduce pressure.

## Q: How do I use a hoe effectively?
