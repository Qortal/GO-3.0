# life-skills — golden FAQ

Curated offline Q&A for Qortino (200 entries). Prefer these answers when relevant.

## Q: What tools belong in a basic home kit?

A: A kit that handles most jobs: claw hammer, multi-bit screwdriver (PH/PZ/flat/Torx), adjustable wrench, locking and needle-nose pliers, utility knife with spare blades, tape measure, spirit level, handsaw, cordless drill with bits, hex keys, duct and electrical tape, super glue and epoxy, wood glue, penetrating oil plus lubricating oil, screws/nails/wall plugs, zip ties, wire, sandpaper, work gloves, safety glasses, and a headlamp. Add a stud/cable finder before drilling walls and a voltage tester before any electrical work.

Also asked as: essential home tools · basic toolkit list · home repair tools

## Q: What is the difference between penetrating oil and lubricating oil?

A: Penetrating oil (like WD-40-type sprays) creeps into rusted threads and frees stuck parts. Lubricating oil stays put and keeps moving parts moving. They are not the same job — free first with penetrant, then protect with real oil so the problem does not return.

Also asked as: WD-40 vs oil · penetrant vs lubricant · freeing stuck bolts

## Q: Why should I own a voltage tester pen?

A: A non-contact voltage tester lets you confirm a circuit is dead before you touch wires or fittings. Always test the tester on a known live outlet first, then on the work, then re-check the known live outlet. Never assume a breaker is off until you have verified it at the point of work.

Also asked as: electrical tester · check if wire live · safe electrical work

## Q: Which household shutoffs should everyone know?

A: Locate and physically test four controls: main water shutoff (label it), water heater valve, gas shutoff (know which tool it needs and keep that tool nearby), and the main electrical breaker. Everyone in the household should know all four. Practising once in calm weather beats learning during a flood or gas leak.

Also asked as: main water gas electric shutoff · know your shutoffs · 10-minute home audit

## Q: What do I do if a pipe bursts?

A: Shut the main water off first. If water is near wiring or outlets, shut the electricity at the main breaker next. Open taps downhill of the leak to drain remaining pressure, then contain the water with towels and a bucket. Call for help once the house is safer — water off beats mopping while it still sprays.

Also asked as: burst pipe response · water leak emergency steps

## Q: What should I do if I smell gas?

A: Do not flip switches, light flames, or use phones inside. Get everyone out, turn the gas valve off only if you can reach it safely on the way, then call the emergency gas line from outside. Ventilate from outdoors if instructed. Treat any strong gas smell as urgent, not something to investigate with a lighter.

Also asked as: gas leak smell steps · gas emergency at home

## Q: Where is the main water shutoff usually found?

A: Often near where the supply enters the building — basement, utility cupboard, under the kitchen sink, or in a meter box outside. Turn it only as far as it will go without forcing; if it is stiff, work it gently a few times a year so it does not seize. Know whether yours is a lever (90°) or a multi-turn valve.

Also asked as: find main water stopcock · locate stop valve

## Q: Why shut electricity if water is near wiring?

A: Water and energised circuits are a shock and fire risk. If a leak, flood, or spray is approaching outlets, switches, or appliances, cut power at the main before wading in. Never stand in water to unplug something that may still be live.

Also asked as: water near outlets danger · flood electrical safety

## Q: How do I unclog a drain without harsh chemicals?

A: Start with a cup plunger: enough water to cover the cup, firm seals, and steady thrusts. If that fails, place a bucket under the U-bend (trap), loosen the slip nuts, empty the trap, and clear debris. Skip caustic drain openers — they rarely finish the job and make later hand work dangerous.

Also asked as: plunger drain clog · clear sink trap · unclog without caustic

## Q: How do I stop a running toilet?

A: Lift the lid and watch: a worn flapper that does not seal, or a float set too high so water never stops, causes most continuous runs. Adjust the float so the water stops 2–3 cm below the overflow tube, or replace the flapper. A running toilet can waste hundreds of litres a day.

Also asked as: toilet keeps running · flapper float adjustment

## Q: How do I replace a tap washer or cartridge?

A: Shut the water to that tap or the main, open the tap to drain pressure, then dismantle in order — photograph each layer with your phone or paper sketch. Replace the washer or cartridge with the matching size, reassemble hand-tight plus a snug finish, and restore water slowly while watching for leaks.

Also asked as: fix dripping tap · tap washer replacement

## Q: What car basics should every driver practise?

A: Practise changing a wheel at home: jack points, loosen nuts before lifting, and lower fully before final tightening in a star pattern. Learn correct jump-start cable order, check oil and coolant when the engine is cool, and replace wipers and bulbs before they fail in rain. Confidence on the driveway beats learning on a dark roadside.

Also asked as: change wheel jump start · basic car skills

## Q: Why avoid chemical drain cleaners?

A: Caustic and acid cleaners can burn skin, damage pipes, and leave a hazardous soup if you later open the trap by hand. Mechanical clearing — plunger, auger, trap removal — solves the clog and stays safer for the next person who works on the pipe.

Also asked as: caustic drain cleaner danger · why not drain acid

## Q: How do I reset a tripped breaker safely?

A: Move the breaker fully to Off, then back to On. If it trips again immediately, leave it off and unplug devices on that circuit before retrying once. Repeated trips mean overload or a fault — find the cause rather than holding the breaker on.

Also asked as: breaker keeps tripping · reset circuit breaker

## Q: How do I sharpen a kitchen knife with a whetstone?

A: Use a two-sided stone: coarse (~400–1000 grit) to set the edge, fine (~3000+) to polish. Hold a consistent ~20° angle, edge trailing, until a burr forms along the other side; flip, repeat, then strip the burr and finish on a strop or cardboard. Wet the stone as its type requires, and go slow — angle consistency beats speed.

Also asked as: whetstone knife sharpening · sharpen knife 20 degrees

## Q: Why is a sharp knife safer than a dull one?

A: A sharp edge bites where you aim with less force. A dull edge skids off skins and boards into fingers. Keep knives sharp, cut with fingers curled, and store blades sheathed or on a magnetic strip — not loose in a drawer.

Also asked as: sharp knife safer · dull knife slips

## Q: How do I maintain cast-iron cookware?

A: Wash with hot water and a brush (minimal soap if well seasoned), dry thoroughly on low heat, then wipe a very thin oil coat while warm. Never soak or leave wet. Thin polymerised layers of oil are seasoning — sticky thick oil is not.

Also asked as: season cast iron · cast iron care never soak

## Q: What belongs in a basic sewing kit?

A: Needles in a few sizes, strong polyester thread (black, white, and a colour you wear), patches, safety pins, spare buttons, fabric scissors, and a thimble. Add fabric glue, seam sealer, and repair tape for waterproof gear. That small kit covers most clothing and bag failures.

Also asked as: home sewing kit contents · mending kit essentials

## Q: How do I sew a button back on?

A: Anchor the thread with a few stitches through the fabric, sew through each button hole 4–6 times leaving a slight thread shank for thick coats, then wind thread under the button and knot behind. Match thread colour and double the thread for strength. A missing button is a ten-minute fix, not a reason to discard the garment.

Also asked as: sew on button · reattach button

## Q: How do I patch clothing so the repair holds?

A: For anything that takes stress — knees, backpacks, work trousers — back an iron-on patch with stitches around the edge. Clean fabric, round the patch corners so they peel less, and stitch with a small running or whip stitch. Iron-on alone is temporary under real load.

Also asked as: patch clothes properly · iron-on patch tips

## Q: What basic cleaners replace a cupboard of products?

A: White vinegar (descaling, glass, fabric softener, weed spray), baking soda (scouring, odors, drain refresh with vinegar), citric acid (kettles, bathroom scale), soap flakes or Castile soap (nearly everything), washing soda (laundry booster, degreasing), and isopropyl alcohol (disinfecting, sticker residue). Cheap, long-storing staples beat branded bottles for most jobs.

Also asked as: vinegar baking soda cleaning · simple household chemistry · DIY cleaners list

## Q: What cleaners should I never mix?

A: Never mix bleach with vinegar, other acids, or ammonia — the fumes can seriously harm lungs and eyes. Use one product, rinse, then another if needed. Simple soap and water remain the default for ordinary dirt.

Also asked as: never mix bleach vinegar · dangerous cleaner combinations

## Q: How do I bake simple bread with only flour, water, and salt?

A: Mix flour, water, salt, and a little yeast or sourdough starter into a shaggy dough; let time develop gluten (no-knead styles rest many hours). Bake in a hot pot or on a stone until the crust is deep brown and the loaf sounds hollow. Practise now so you can bake when shops are closed.

Also asked as: no knead bread basics · simple homemade bread

## Q: How do I keep a sourdough starter without buying yeast?

A: A starter is flour plus water plus patience — feed it regularly with equal parts (by weight if you can) and keep a portion going. Once active, it leavens bread indefinitely without commercial yeast. If you refrigerate between bakes, feed it and let it warm and bubble before mixing dough.

Also asked as: sourdough starter care · wild yeast starter

## Q: How do I cook without a fridge for a few days?

A: Favour shelf-stable staples: rice, pasta, dried beans, oats, flour, oil, salt, hard cheeses if cool, whole winter squash, onions, garlic, potatoes in a dark ventilated place, and tinned goods. Cook what spoils first, use cool night air and shade, and trust your nose and eyes — when in doubt, heat thoroughly or discard.

Also asked as: no fridge cooking · food without refrigeration

## Q: How do I stretch meals from pantry staples?

A: Beans-and-rice, soups, and stews turn small amounts of flavouring vegetables, spices, and fat into filling meals. Soak beans when you can, cook thoroughly, and salt near the end. One pot feeding many is the classic resilience pattern — practise favourite spice combinations before you need them.

Also asked as: beans and rice meals · soup stew from storage

## Q: Why cook every stored recipe before you need it?

A: Unfamiliar grains, yeast habits, and cooker quirks show up only when you practise. Cooking from your storage pantry now teaches timing, water ratios, and what your household will actually eat. Crisis is a poor first kitchen lesson.

Also asked as: practise storage recipes · test emergency meals

## Q: How do I wash clothes by hand?

A: Sort lights and darks, dissolve soap in a basin of water, soak, then agitate and rub soiled areas against themselves. Rinse until water runs clearer, wring or press out water, and hang in air and sun when possible. Hot water helps grease; cold is fine for many daily clothes and saves fuel.

Also asked as: hand wash laundry · wash clothes without machine

## Q: What basic knots should everyone know?

A: Learn square (reef) knot for joining similar cords under light load, bowline for a fixed loop that does not slip, clove hitch for tying to a post, taut-line hitch for adjustable guy lines, and a simple stopper knot so rope ends do not run. Practise until you can tie them in poor light. A few reliable knots beat a dozen half-remembered ones.

Also asked as: essential knots list · beginner knot skills

## Q: How do I tie a bowline?

A: Make a small loop in the standing part (“the rabbit hole”), bring the working end up through the hole, around the standing part, and back down the hole; snug carefully. A bowline makes a secure loop at the end of a rope for rescue, hanging, or tying around an object. Practise the rhyme until muscle memory holds.

Also asked as: tie bowline knot · fixed loop knot

## Q: How do I read a topographic map basics?

A: Contour lines connect equal elevation — tightly packed lines mean steep ground; wide spacing means gentle slopes. Read the scale bar in metres/kilometres, note the north arrow, and match symbols to the legend. Orient the map to the land before you navigate.

Also asked as: read topo map · contour lines meaning

## Q: How do I orient a map with a compass?

A: Place the compass on the map, rotate the map until map north matches magnetic north (adjust for local declination if you know it), and keep both aligned as you plan. Thumb and terrain features confirm you are reading the right ridge. A map that is upside-down to the land causes classic wrong turns.

Also asked as: orient map compass · map north alignment

## Q: What is a map scale and why does it matter?

A: Scale tells you how ground distance relates to map distance — for example 1:25,000 means 1 cm on the map is 250 m on the ground. Always use the printed scale bar, which survives photocopy resizing better than assumptions. Underestimating scale is how short walks become epics.

Also asked as: map scale bar · 1:25000 map meaning

## Q: How do I take a simple compass bearing?

A: Point the travel arrow at your target, rotate the bezel until north marks align with the needle (with declination set if your compass allows), then follow the arrow while keeping the needle boxed. Pick interim aiming points so you are not staring at the dial every step. Recheck after obstacles.

Also asked as: take compass bearing · follow a bearing walk

## Q: How should I think about a household financial buffer?

A: Build layers: small-denomination cash at home for outages and local trade, a modest emergency fund for bills when income hiccups, and lower recurring costs so less income still covers life. Frugality is resilience, not punishment — every fixed bill you remove is weight off your back in hard months.

Also asked as: emergency fund layers · cash buffer resilience

## Q: Why keep cash at home in small notes?

A: Card networks and ATMs fail in outages; large notes are hard to change when shops are stressed. Keep a discreet, modest cash reserve in small denominations for food, fuel, and transport. Tell only trusted household members where it is.

Also asked as: emergency cash home · small bills power outage

## Q: What is a simple offline household budget method?

A: List monthly must-pays (housing, water, food staples, medicine, debt minimums), then needs, then wants. Use envelopes or labelled jars for cash categories if that helps your brain. Review weekly with pencil and paper — awareness beats fancy apps you cannot open without power.

Also asked as: envelope budget cash · simple spending plan

## Q: What is a healthy barter mindset?

A: Trade surplus for surplus — skills, labour, eggs, repairs, childcare hours — with clear agreements and goodwill. Fairness and reputation matter more than squeezing the last unit of value. Barter thrives on trust; cheating once can close many doors.

Also asked as: barter fair trade · skill exchange neighbours

## Q: How do I prioritise spending in a tight month?

A: Secure shelter, water, basic food, essential medicine, and heat/light before discretionary spending. Talk with the household so everyone understands the temporary rules. Panic purchases and shame spending both blow holes in thin budgets.

Also asked as: tight budget priorities · pay what first

## Q: Why are neighbours part of emergency preparedness?

A: In real emergencies, nearby people are the actual first responders — sharing tools, information, care, and muscle. Know names, skills, and needs on your street before crisis. Isolation is fragility; trust built in peacetime is infrastructure.

Also asked as: neighbours emergency resource · community as infrastructure

## Q: How do I start knowing my neighbours’ skills?

A: Casual conversations and small favours reveal who gardens, who wires, who minds kids, who speaks extra languages. Share your own skills first so it feels like exchange, not interrogation. A simple paper list at home helps when phones are down.

Also asked as: skill map neighbours · ask neighbours skills

## Q: How do I borrow and lend tools without losing friends?

A: Return cleaner and sooner than promised, replace consumables you used (blades, bits), and confess damage immediately with a repair or replace plan. Label your tools. Clear expectations keep generosity alive.

Also asked as: lend tools etiquette · borrow tool return

## Q: How should a street communicate if phones fail?

A: Agree a meeting point, a paper contact list, and who checks on whom — especially elders and anyone with medical needs (non-medical check-ins). A whistle, knock code, or scheduled porch check can be enough. Practise once so it is not theoretical.

Also asked as: phone down neighbour contact · local info tree

## Q: What documents belong in a household binder?

A: ID copies, insurance policies, property documents, medical summaries and prescriptions, and key contacts — with a waterproof copy in the go-bag and an encrypted digital copy on offline USB (Faraday storage if you use that setup) plus one off-site copy. One binder beats a scavenger hunt during eviction, fire, or travel.

Also asked as: important papers binder · document go bag copies

## Q: How should I back up irreplaceable digital files?

A: Follow 3-2-1: three copies, on two different media types, with one copy off-site. Include photos, documents, and recovery codes. Test that you can open a backup once a year. Cloud-only is not a plan when networks fail.

Also asked as: 3-2-1 backup rule · offline backup photos documents

## Q: How do I store password recovery codes offline?

A: Use a password manager for daily use, then print recovery codes and store them with your document binder — not on a sticky note on the monitor. Anyone with physical access to codes can become you; protect them like cash and ID.

Also asked as: printed password recovery · password manager paper codes

## Q: What medical information should be on paper at home?

A: A one-page summary: conditions, medications with doses, allergies, blood type if known, and clinician contacts. Keep a copy in the binder and go-bag. This is documentation for continuity — not a substitute for professional care.

Also asked as: paper medical summary · prescription list copy

## Q: What contact list should every household keep on paper?

A: Family, neighbours, landlord, utilities, insurance, doctors, school/work, and out-of-area contact who can relay messages. Include addresses, not only phone numbers. Phones die; paper still reads by candlelight.

Also asked as: paper contact list · emergency phone numbers paper

## Q: Why is bicycle repair a life skill?

A: A working bicycle is quiet, repairable transport when fuel and transit fail. Learning punctures, brakes, and chain care keeps you mobile with hand tools. Practise before you depend on it.

Also asked as: bike grid down transport · bicycle preparedness

## Q: How do I fix a bicycle puncture?

A: Remove the wheel, lever the tyre carefully, find the hole by air and ears or dunking the tube, roughen, apply patch kit glue as directed, press patch, replace tyre without pinching the tube, and inflate firmly. Check the tyre casing for the thorn or glass that caused it before you celebrate.

Also asked as: repair bike puncture · patch bike tube

## Q: How do I adjust bicycle brakes?

A: Centre the callipers or pads so they hit the rim (or disc) squarely without rubbing when released; tighten cable anchor bolts after pulling slack. Replace pads worn to the indicator. Test at walking speed before riding downhill. Strong brakes are non-negotiable safety kit.

Also asked as: adjust bike brakes · brake pads rubbing

## Q: How do I care for a bicycle chain?

A: Wipe grit off, apply a suitable chain lubricant to each link while turning pedals backwards, then wipe excess so it does not attract dirt. A dry, squeaking chain wears faster; a dripping oily chain paints your trousers and grime. Recheck after wet rides.

Also asked as: lube bike chain · clean bicycle chain

## Q: What tools belong in a small bike repair kit?

A: Tyre levers, patch kit or spare tube, mini pump or CO₂ you know how to use, multi-tool with hex keys and screwdrivers, and a rag. Add a spare chain quick-link if you ride far. Know how to use each tool at home before the roadside lesson.

Also asked as: bike tool kit basics · cycle puncture kit

## Q: How do I keep a calmer household during stressful times?

A: Protect sleep and meals as anchors, keep daily routines simple and visible, reduce news noise indoors, and give everyone one useful job so anxiety has somewhere to go. Speak lower and shorter when tension rises. Calm is contagious — so is panic.

Also asked as: calm home routines stress · household peace tips

## Q: How do I set household rules kids can follow offline?

A: Few rules, clearly worded, consistently kept: where we meet if separated, shoes by the door, ask before touching tools/fire, kindness in words. Post them on paper at child eye level. Consistency from adults matters more than a long list.

Also asked as: clear family rules kids · simple house rules

## Q: How do I help children with big feelings without screens?

A: Name the feeling, stay nearby, offer water/snack/rest, and a simple physical outlet (walk, cushions, drawing). Connection before correction. You are not diagnosing — you are being a steady presence while the storm passes.

Also asked as: kids big feelings offline · co regulation children

## Q: How do I talk to children about hard world events calmly?

A: Tell the truth briefly at their level, emphasise what adults are doing to keep them safe, and invite questions. Limit graphic repeated exposure. Reassurance is honesty plus a plan, not pretend nothing happened.

Also asked as: explain crisis to kids · honest calm parenting talk

## Q: How do I manage adult stress so it spills less onto children?

A: Take short resets: water, breath outdoors, swap duties with another adult when possible, and sleep as a priority. Apologise when you snap — repair teaches children that relationships can mend. Your nervous system sets the household weather.

Also asked as: parent stress management · adults regulate first

## Q: What is the meta-skill of practical learning?

A: Fix or learn one new practical thing each month with a manual or careful practice before calling a professional when safety allows. Competence compounds — each skill makes the next easier. Keep paper references: repair manuals, first aid, regional plant guides, and this kind of handbook.

Also asked as: fix one thing a month · competence compounds

## Q: How do I learn a hands-on skill from a paper manual?

A: Read the whole procedure once, gather tools, then do each step slowly while checking the diagram. Write your own notes in the margin about what differed in your house. Teaching the skill to someone else the next week locks it in.

Also asked as: learn from repair manual · study skill offline

## Q: Why keep paper reference books at home?

A: When power and networks fail, paper still works. A repair manual, first-aid guide, regional plant guide, and cookbook earn their shelf space. Annotate them with local phone numbers and your own discoveries.

Also asked as: paper references offline · books when internet down

## Q: How do I practise skills so they stick under stress?

A: Rehearse in realistic conditions: dim light, gloves, time limits, and with the actual tools from your kit. Spaced practice beats one long cram. If you only ever watched someone else do it, you do not know it yet.

Also asked as: deliberate practice skills · train like you use

## Q: What is the role of mistakes in learning life skills?

A: Choose practice projects where failure is cheap and safe — scrap wood, old shirts, flat trails. Mistakes in that sandbox are tuition. On gas, electricity, structural walls, and life-safety knots, stay humble and get skilled help until you are sure.

Also asked as: mistakes while learning repairs · fail safely practise

## Q: How do I check smoke and carbon monoxide alarms offline?

A: Press the test button monthly, replace batteries on a fixed calendar if not sealed long-life units, and replace units by the manufacturer expiry. Alarms are life skills infrastructure — silent alarms help nobody. Know the different sound patterns if your units distinguish smoke and CO.

Also asked as: test smoke alarm · CO alarm monthly check

## Q: What daily habits quietly build life-skill competence?

A: Put tools back, notice small leaks early, cook from staples weekly, mend soon after damage, greet neighbours, and learn one micro-skill at a time. Preparedness is mostly ordinary habits done consistently — not a bunker fantasy. Start where you stand tonight.

Also asked as: daily life skill habits · everyday preparedness habits

## Q: When do I need a stud finder?

A: Use a stud/cable finder before drilling into walls so you hit framing when you need strength and avoid cables and pipes when you do not. Mark both edges of the stud, drill a small pilot hole, and stop if you feel unexpected resistance or see dust that looks like plastic insulation.

Also asked as: cable finder before drilling · find studs in wall · safe wall drilling

## Q: What safety gear belongs with every tool kit?

A: Keep work gloves, safety glasses, and a headlamp with the tools — not in another room. Eyes and hands are the usual casualties of rushed jobs. A headlamp frees both hands and lights the work instead of casting shadows from a handheld torch.

Also asked as: work gloves safety glasses · basic PPE for home repairs

## Q: How should I store hand tools so they last?

A: Wipe tools clean after use, dry them, and wipe metal with a light film of oil. Hang or rack them so edges are protected and moisture does not sit in a closed damp box. Wooden handles get a yearly coat of linseed oil so they do not crack and splinter.

Also asked as: tool care storage · stop tools rusting · oil tools

## Q: Why keep both duct tape and electrical tape?

A: Duct tape is for temporary mechanical holds and sealing drafts — not for live electrical joints. Electrical tape is for insulating and bundling wires where heat and flex matter. Using the wrong tape can fail under load or create a fire risk; keep both and use each for its job.

Also asked as: duct vs electrical tape · which tape for repairs

## Q: Why keep spare utility knife blades?

A: A sharp blade cuts where you aim with less force; a dull one slips. Swap blades when cutting cardboard or tape starts to tear instead of slice, and dispose of used blades in a sealed tin. Never leave an open knife in a toolbox where hands reach blindly.

Also asked as: dull blade danger · utility knife blades

## Q: How do I label home shutoffs clearly?

A: Use waterproof tags or paint-marker labels at each valve and breaker: Main Water, Water Heater, Gas, Main Power. Add a simple arrow for open/closed if the handle is confusing. A torch and a laminated card by the door listing locations helps guests and children act without searching.

Also asked as: label water valve · tag shutoff valves

## Q: Why keep the gas shutoff tool next to the valve?

A: Some gas valves need a specific key or wrench. If that tool lives in a drawer upstairs, you will not find it in the dark during an emergency. Keep the correct tool tied or hung at the valve, and show every adult where it is during your shutoff drill.

Also asked as: gas meter key location · gas shutoff tool

## Q: What is the difference between a room breaker and the main breaker?

A: Individual breakers cut power to one circuit (lights, kitchen, etc.). The main breaker kills everything from the panel. For floods near wiring or major electrical smells, use the main. For a single faulty appliance circuit, switching that one breaker is enough and keeps the rest of the house usable.

Also asked as: main breaker vs circuit breaker · electrical panel basics

## Q: How often should I test shutoff valves?

A: Turn each critical valve fully off and back on once or twice a year so seals do not stick. Do this when you can tolerate a short water or heating interruption. If a valve will not move, do not muscle it until it snaps — plan a replacement before you need it in a crisis.

Also asked as: exercise shutoff valves · prevent seized stopcock

## Q: What should children know about household emergencies?

A: Age-appropriately: where to meet outside, how to leave without hunting for shoes, and who to tell. Older children can learn which adult turns water or gas and that they must not touch electrical panels alone. Calm rehearsal builds competence; fear lectures do not.

Also asked as: teach kids shutoffs · family emergency roles

## Q: How do I patch a small drywall or plasterboard hole?

A: For small holes, seat a self-adhesive mesh patch or backer, spread thin layers of joint compound, let each dry, then sand flush and paint. Deep holes need a scrap backing screwed or glued behind the opening first. Thin coats beat one thick blob that cracks and shrinks.

Also asked as: patch plasterboard hole · fix wall hole

## Q: How do I fix a door that sticks?

A: Tighten hinge screws first — a longer screw into the frame often pulls the door back into alignment. Check for swollen wood after damp weather and plane or sand the rub point lightly if needed. Confirm the latch meets the strike plate before you cut anything permanently.

Also asked as: sticking door repair · door rubs frame

## Q: How do I bleed a radiator?

A: With the heating on, hold a rag under the bleed valve and open it slowly with a radiator key until the hiss of air becomes a steady water trickle, then close. Top up system pressure if your boiler gauge drops below the marked range. Cold tops with hot bottoms usually mean trapped air.

Also asked as: bleed heating radiator · radiator cold at top

## Q: What plug and fuse skills are worth learning?

A: Where local rules allow DIY: learn correct wiring colours for your region, firm cord grip, and the right fuse rating for the appliance. If you are unsure, stop and use a qualified person — electricity mistakes are not practice projects. A voltage tester and power off are non-negotiable before opening anything.

Also asked as: rewire plug basics · replace fuse safely

## Q: How do I use a plunger correctly?

A: Choose a flange plunger for toilets and a cup plunger for sinks. Seal the overflow on sinks with a wet cloth, keep water over the cup, and push-pull with steady force rather than splashing. Ten controlled strokes often beat random slamming.

Also asked as: plunger technique · plunge toilet or sink

## Q: How do I hang a heavy shelf safely?

A: Find studs and drive screws into timber framing whenever the load is real books or tools. Use rated anchors only when studs are impossible, and match anchor type to wall material. A spirit level and two-person lift prevent the “almost level” shelf that creeps over months.

Also asked as: mount shelf on studs · heavy shelf fixing

## Q: How do I stop a minor leak under a sink temporarily?

A: Shut water, dry the area, and use a rubber patch with a hose clamp or proper repair tape rated for plumbing as a short-term hold. Buckets and a phone call for parts beat ignoring drips that ruin cabinets. Temporary fixes are for hours or days, not permanent plumbing.

Also asked as: temp fix pipe leak · compression leak stopgap

## Q: How do I replace a broken light bulb safely?

A: Power off at the switch (or breaker for stubborn fixtures), let the bulb cool, and use a dry cloth or glove for grip. For glass that breaks in the socket, power must stay off — press a raw potato or folded cardboard into the base to unscrew, or use a proper bulb extractor. Match wattage and fitting type.

Also asked as: change light bulb safe · stuck bulb removal

## Q: How do I fix a running or dripping outdoor tap in frost season?

A: Drain outdoor taps before hard frost, isolate indoor valves feeding them, and leave the outdoor valve open after draining so residual water can expand without cracking. Insulate exposed pipes where you can. A cracked outdoor tap in spring is usually winter water that had nowhere to go.

Also asked as: winter outdoor tap · prevent outdoor tap freeze

## Q: How do I sharpen an axe or garden spade?

A: Secure the tool, then push a file along the existing bevel in one direction to restore a clean edge — axes and spades usually want a slightly blunter angle than kitchen knives. Deburr lightly, then oil the metal. Digging tools cut roots and soil cleaner when edged, which also means less strain on your body.

Also asked as: sharpen axe file · file garden tools

## Q: How do I care for wooden tool handles?

A: Sand raised grain lightly, wipe on boiled linseed oil, let it soak, then wipe dry. Yearly oiling keeps handles from drying, cracking, and blistering your hands. Replace any handle with a deep split before it fails mid-swing.

Also asked as: linseed oil handles · prevent cracked tool handles

## Q: How do I stop rust on hand tools?

A: Dry tools after damp work, store with airflow, and wipe a light oil film on bare steel. Light rust yields to fine abrasive and oil; heavy scale may need a wire brush then re-oil. Silica gel in a closed toolbox helps in humid climates.

## Q: How do I whip-stitch a seam that has opened?

A: Turn the garment inside out, align the seam edges, and sew over-and-over stitches that bite both edges evenly. Knot firmly at each end. Whip stitch is quick insurance until you can do a neater seam; for load-bearing splits, backstitch or machine sew when you can.

## Q: How do I darn a sock?

A: Stretch the sock over a jar lid or darning egg, stitch a grid of threads across the hole, then weave across that grid until the hole is filled. Keep tension even so the darn is flat. Wool socks especially reward repair and can last years.

## Q: How do I tie a square (reef) knot?
