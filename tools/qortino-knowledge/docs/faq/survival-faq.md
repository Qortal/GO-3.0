## Q: How much water should I store per person per day?

A: Plan at least 4 litres per person per day: about 2 for drinking and 2 for cooking and basic hygiene. In heat, hard work, illness, or pregnancy, drinking alone can need 4–6 litres. Two weeks per person is a solid household baseline — a family of four needs roughly 112 litres for one week.

## Q: How do I store water safely at home?

A: Use food-grade containers only (HDPE #2 jugs, 200 L drums, or factory-sealed bottled water). Store cool and dark, off concrete on wood slats. Never reuse milk jugs. Tap water from a treated municipal supply can store as-is; rotate yearly. For well water, add 2 drops of unscented 5–6% bleach per litre when filling.

## Q: How do I purify water by boiling?

A: 1. Bring water to a rolling boil.
2. Keep it boiling 1 minute (3 minutes above about 2,000 m elevation).
3. Let it cool covered before drinking.
Boiling kills bacteria, protozoa, and viruses — the gold standard when you have fuel.
Do NOT skip the full boil time at altitude, where water boils cooler.

## Q: How do I treat water with household bleach?

A: 1. Use unscented 5–6% household bleach only.
2. Add 2 drops per litre of clear water, or 4 drops per litre if cloudy (after settling/pre-filtering).
3. Mix and wait 30–60 minutes before drinking.
Bleach helps against bacteria and viruses but is only partial against Cryptosporidium.
Do NOT use scented, colour-safe, or cleaning blends — only plain bleach.

## Q: Do hollow-fiber water filters remove viruses?

A: A good hollow-fiber filter (about 0.1 micron) removes bacteria and protozoa (Giardia/Crypto) but typically does NOT remove viruses. Pair it with boiling or a chemical that covers viruses (chlorine dioxide or bleach for many viruses), or use a true purifier bottle. Protect hollow-fiber filters from freezing — cracked fibres can pass microbes with no visible damage. With no fire: filter first, then use chemical treatment or a purifier — do not rely on clear looks alone.

## Q: What is the best layered water treatment kit?

A: Cover three layers: a gravity or squeeze hollow-fiber filter for volume, chlorine dioxide tablets as compact backup (best chemical option; wait up to 4 hours for Crypto), and the knowledge plus fuel to boil. Mark dedicated dirty and clean buckets permanently to avoid cross-contamination.

## Q: How do I make muddy river water drinkable?

A: 1. Pre-filter through cloth, a coffee filter, or a Millbank bag, or let sediment settle and decant.
2. Then boil, filter with a hollow-fiber unit, and/or use chlorine dioxide.
3. Prefer moving water, collected from the surface upstream of trails and livestock.
Sediment shelters microbes and clogs filters.
Do NOT drink untreated surface water no matter how clear — Giardia is invisible.

## Q: Can I drink rainwater from my roof?

A: Rain catchment is useful: roughly 1 litre per square metre per millimetre of rain (a 20 m² roof in a 10 mm shower ≈ 200 L). First-flush the initial runoff that carries roof grime and bird droppings, then still treat before drinking (boil, filter, or chemical). Use clean tarp or metal roof sections into food-grade barrels.

## Q: Is urine or salt water drinkable in survival?

A: Salt water and urine are never drinkable as-is — both dehydrate you further. Distillation is the only practical way to reclaim usable water from them. Focus on finding fresh sources, melting snow properly, rain catchment, or treating surface water instead.

## Q: How do I get water from snow or ice?

A: Melt snow with a little starter liquid in the pot so it does not scorch. Ice yields more water per volume than snow. In cold conditions, never eat snow directly — it lowers core temperature. Once melted, treat as needed (boil is most reliable). Keep hollow-fiber filters warm against your body so they do not freeze.

## Q: Should I ration water in the heat?

A: Ration sweat, not water: rest in shade through midday, work at dawn and dusk, and drink what you have. People have died of dehydration with water still in their canteens. Shade, pacing, and treating every available source beat sipping tiny amounts while still sweating hard.

## Q: How do chlorine dioxide tablets compare to bleach?

A: Chlorine dioxide tablets are the best chemical option for field use: they cover bacteria, viruses, and Cryptosporidium (often needing up to about 4 hours for Crypto). Bleach is cheap and common but only partial against Crypto. Keep tablets as compact backup even if you mainly boil or filter.

## Q: What water sources should I prefer in the wild?

A: Prefer moving water over stagnant. Collect from the surface, not a stirred-up bottom, and upstream of trails, livestock, and settlements. Always treat before drinking. Avoid salt water and anything you cannot distill. Clarity means nothing — treat every wild source.

## Q: How do I protect a water filter in winter?

A: Freezing destroys hollow-fiber filters — cracked fibres can pass microbes with no visible damage. Sleep with the filter in your jacket, keep it against your body when hiking, or switch to boiling and chemicals in hard freezes. Never assume a thawed filter is still safe if it froze solid.

## Q: What elevation changes boiling time for water?

A: At low elevation, a rolling boil for 1 minute is the usual standard. Above about 2,000 m, boil 3 minutes because water boils at a lower temperature. When fuel is scarce, a quality filter plus chemical backup can save fuel, but boiling remains the most foolproof microbial kill.

## Q: What is the survival rule of threes?

A: Rough priorities: about 3 minutes without air, 3 hours without shelter in harsh exposure, 3 days without water, and 3 weeks without food. In cold, wet climates, shelter and fire often outrank everything else — hypothermia kills far faster than hunger.

## Q: What are the 5 C's of survivability?

A: A common bushcraft framework: (1) Cutting tool — fixed-blade knife; (2) Combustion — ferro rod, lighter, tinder; (3) Cover — tarp/blanket/rain gear; (4) Container — metal bottle or pot you can boil in; (5) Cordage — paracord or bankline. These are hard to improvise well in the field.

## Q: How do I build a reliable fire in the wild?

A: 1. Gather tinder, pencil-thin twigs, then finger- and wrist-thick fuel before striking.
2. Build on dry ground or a stick platform; shield from wind.
3. Light the tinder, then feed gradually up in size.
Carry two ignition sources; a ferro rod still works when wet/cold lighters fail.
Do NOT light first and scramble for fuel — that is how fires die.

## Q: How do I make fire in winter snow?

A: Clear snow to ground or build a raft of green logs so the fire does not melt into a pit. Use standing dead wood (ground wood is soaked), split to dry cores, and process 3–4× more fuel than feels necessary before lighting. Feather sticks and fatwood light when everything else is wet. Warm the ferro rod and your hands first.

## Q: How should I site and build an emergency shelter?

A: 1. Pick a site out of wind, off low ground, away from dead standing trees.
2. Rig a tarp A-frame or lean-to fast.
3. Insulate underneath you — ground conduction steals more heat than air.
A quality emergency blanket or wool layer under/over you matters as much as the roof.
Do NOT soak your clothes with sweat while overbuilding a debris hut in cold weather.

## Q: Why is wool better than cotton in the cold?

A: Never wear cotton against skin when cold — it holds moisture and chills you. Wool (and many synthetics) insulates even when wet; down fails when wet. Wool also resists ember burns near fires and odour over multi-day wear. A sturdy wool blanket can serve as coat, sleep system, stretcher pad, or windbreak.

## Q: How do I signal for help if I am lost?

A: 1. Stop, stay warm, and stay put if safe.
2. Make yourself visible: open ground, bright colours, signal mirror/flashes.
3. Use three of anything — whistle blasts, fires, or light flashes — as a distress pattern.
A whistle carries farther than shouting for far less energy.
Do NOT wander farther looking for a perfect ridge once you are already exhausted and cold.

## Q: How do I navigate without a GPS?

A: Carry a compass and paper map even if you have GPS. Without a compass: sun rises roughly east and sets roughly west; a stick’s shadow tip marked twice gives an east–west line; the North Star (via the Big Dipper pointers) sits due north. Pace-count distance (calibrate your own paces; many adults are ~65 double-paces per 100 m). Look behind you regularly so the return route is memorised.

## Q: What should I do if I get lost outdoors?

A: 1. Stop. Sit, breathe, and prevent panic decisions.
2. Stay warm and dry — shelter and fire before long searches.
3. Mark your location, make yourself visible, and signal (whistle threes).
Tell someone your route and return time before you leave home.
Do NOT keep walking for hours hoping to stumble on a road when night and cold are coming.

## Q: How does ground insulation affect shelter warmth?

A: You lose more heat into the ground than into the air. Always put insulation under you: closed-cell foam, a thick bed of dry debris, packs, or extra clothing. A tarp roof without under-insulation still leaves you cold. Elevate or pad before you add fancy walls.

## Q: How do I gather fuel before lighting a fire?

A: Collect the whole ladder first: fluffy or bark tinder, pencil-thin twigs, finger-thick sticks, then wrist-thick fuel. Light only when the pile is ready and weather-protected. Leaving a baby flame to fetch wood is a classic way to lose the fire — especially in winter.

## Q: What ignition sources should I carry?

A: Carry at least two ignition sources. A butane lighter is convenient; a ferro rod still throws sparks when wet or frozen. Pair both with dry tinder on your body. Bic-style lighters fail in cold until warmed in a pocket.

## Q: How do I use a tarp as a quick shelter?

A: An A-frame or lean-to tarp is the fastest effective shelter for most people. Rig ridge line with cordage, stake corners, and put thick insulation on the ground under your sleep system. Face the open side away from wind and rain. Practice at home so you can do it in the dark.

## Q: What does three whistle blasts mean?

A: Three of anything — whistle blasts, fires, or light flashes — is widely recognised as a distress signal. Carry a loud whistle; it saves voice and energy. Keep signalling on a schedule so rescuers can home in.

## Q: How should I dress in layers for cold weather?

A: Use a moisture-managing base (wool/synthetic, not cotton), an insulating mid layer, and a wind/rain shell. Vent before you sweat; damp clothing destroys insulation. Cover head and hands. Change into dry sleep clothes before bed in the field.

## Q: How do I avoid soaking clothes while building shelter?

A: Debris and snow shelters cost sweat. Vent layers, work at a sustainable pace, and stop to rewarm before you are damp. Wet clothing in wind can turn shelter-building into a hypothermia emergency. Sometimes a simple tarp beats an epic debris hut.

## Q: What should I tell someone before a hike?

A: Share your route, companions, and return time with someone who will notice if you are late. Agree when they should call for help. This single habit saves more lives than exotic gear, because searchers know where to look.

## Q: What makes a fixed-blade knife the top bushcraft tool?

A: A decent fixed-blade carbon-steel knife processes tinder, builds shelter, prepares food, and does camp crafts when folders fail. It is the cutting tool in the 5 C’s because replacing that capability from scratch in the field is hard. Keep it sharp and sheathed.

## Q: What wild plants are safer learning targets?

A: Learn a handful of unmistakable local species from experts — e.g. bilberries/crowberries in some northern regions, and widespread cooked nettles or dandelion greens elsewhere. Harvest away from roads and spray. Never invent tests for unknown plants or mushrooms.

## Q: Why shouldn't I eat unidentified mushrooms?

A: Assume any wild mushroom is unsafe until an expert confirms the exact species — there is no reliable shortcut test. Never experiment. Learn a handful of unmistakable local plants from experts and leave all uncertain fungi alone. Mistakes can be fatal.

## Q: Why keep paper maps if I have offline map apps?

## Q: How do I recognize and treat hypothermia?

A: 1. Get them out of wind and wet clothing; add dry insulation.
2. If fully conscious, give warm sweet drinks; warm the torso with skin-to-skin or heat packs (not extremities first).
3. Handle gently and seek emergency services (112 in Europe) if available — severe hypothermia is life-threatening.
Early signs: intense shivering, clumsy hands, slurred speech, apathy.
Do NOT rub extremities hard, give alcohol, or use hot baths on a semi-conscious person.

## Q: How do I stay warm when home heating fails?

A: Heat the person, then one room, then maybe the house. Wear wool/synthetic base layers, hat and warm socks indoors, use a hot water bottle in a sleeping bag, and gather everyone in the smallest sheltered room with doors closed and blankets over windows. A climate-rated sleeping bag is a heating system that needs no fuel. Safe heat: wood stove, or indoor-rated heaters with ventilation and a working CO alarm.

## Q: What causes carbon monoxide poisoning in blackouts?

A: Carbon monoxide from combustion kills quietly every winter. Never heat or cook with a barbecue, camp stove, open oven, or generator indoors or in a garage. Generators stay outdoors, 5+ m from windows, exhaust pointed away. Indoor-rated paraffin/propane heaters still need a cracked window and a working CO alarm with fresh batteries.

Also asked as: CO poisoning generator · never run generator indoors · carbon monoxide heater danger

## Q: What are the signs of carbon monoxide poisoning?

A: 1. Headache, nausea, dizziness, confusion, or flu-like symptoms with combustion appliances running — get everyone outside to fresh air immediately.
2. Turn off the source if safe; ventilate.
3. Call emergency services if anyone is altered, fainting, or not improving.
CO is odourless.
Do NOT go back inside to "sleep it off" next to the heater or generator.

## Q: What light sources are best in a blackout?

A: Priority: LED headlamps (hands-free; 50–150 hours on low), then LED lanterns, then flame (candles/oil lamps) with hard rules — stable holders, away from curtains, never asleep or unattended, plus smoke alarms and an extinguisher. Prefer lithium primary AA/AAA (10–20 year shelf life); alkalines leak in storage. Add solar recharge when possible.

## Q: How do I handle toilet sanitation when water is out?

A: If sewers are intact, flush manually with 6–8 L of non-potable water poured into the bowl at once. If sewage may back up or water is precious: twin buckets — urine separate; feces in a lined bucket covered with sawdust, ash, or dry soil after each use. Wash hands every time. Store sealed waste away from living areas; bury later 20+ cm deep and 60+ m from water if authorities allow.

## Q: Why is handwashing critical in disasters?

A: In historical grid-down events, hygiene-related illness dwarfed injury. Most disaster diseases are fecal–oral and stop at clean hands. Set a wash station (jug with tap or pierced-cap bottle, soap, towel) by the toilet bucket and kitchen. Alcohol gel (60%+) is backup, not a full substitute when hands are soiled.

## Q: Are candles safe for blackout heat?

A: Candles and oil lamps are for light and morale, not primary heat. Use stable holders, keep away from curtains, never leave them asleep or unattended, and keep a fire extinguisher and smoke alarm per level. Glow sticks are child-safe night lights with zero fire risk. Headlamps beat open flame for task light.

## Q: What batteries should I store for emergencies?

A: Lithium primary AA/AAA cells store 10–20 years without leaking. Alkalines often leak and ruin devices in long storage. Rechargeables plus a small solar panel make light and radios self-sustaining. Standardise sizes across headlamps and radios to simplify logistics.

## Q: How do I cool a burn in the field?

A: 1. Cool with running cool (not ice) water for 20 minutes.
2. Cover loosely with a clean non-fluffy dressing.
3. For large, deep, facial, genital, or circumferential burns — seek emergency care urgently.
This is educational first aid, not a substitute for professional care.
Do NOT apply butter, toothpaste, ice packs directly, or puncture large blisters as "treatment."

## Q: What is the one-warm-room strategy?

A: Pick the smallest room on the sheltered side of the house, close doors, hang blankets over doorways and windows, and gather people, pets, and safe light sources there. Body heat alone can hold a small sealed room several degrees above the rest of the house. Sleep in bags rated for your climate.

## Q: Can I heat my house with a charcoal barbecue indoors?

A: Never. Barbecues, camp stoves, ovens left open, and generators used indoors produce carbon monoxide that kills without a strong smell warning. Only use heating appliances rated for indoor use, with ventilation and a working CO alarm. When in doubt, add clothing and sleeping bags instead of burning fuel inside.

## Q: How do I set up an emergency handwashing station?

A: Place a jug with a tap or a bottle with a pierced cap, soap, and a towel beside the toilet bucket and the kitchen. Make handwashing automatic after toilet use and before food. Alcohol gel (60%+) backs it up when water is scarce but does not remove heavy soil alone.

## Q: What is the twin-bucket toilet system?

A: One bucket for urine (lower hazard; dilute and dispose away from water). One lined bucket for feces, covered after each use with sawdust, ash, dry soil, or shredded paper to control odour and flies. Seal, store away from living space, wash hands every time. This prevents disease when toilets fail.

## Q: How do I manually flush a toilet with no water service?

A: If sewer lines are intact, pour 6–8 litres of non-potable water (rain, pool, ditch) into the bowl in one confident pour to siphon-flush. Do not waste drinking water on this if sewers may back up — switch to the twin-bucket system instead.

## Q: How do I safely use an indoor-rated propane heater?

## Q: Why avoid cotton base layers when cold and wet?

A: Cotton holds water against skin and accelerates heat loss — a fast path toward hypothermia in wind. Choose wool or synthetics next to skin for cold weather work and sleep systems. Save cotton for hot, dry chores if you must.

## Q: How do I treat heat illness in the field?

A: 1. Stop activity; move to shade or a cool place.
2. Loosen clothing; cool with wet cloths; sip safe fluids with electrolytes if available.
3. If confused, fainting, or extremely hot skin — this can be life-threatening: cool aggressively and seek emergency services if reachable.
Do NOT force someone who is confused to drink large amounts, and do not keep working through severe symptoms.

## Q: How do I stop severe bleeding?

A: 1. Apply hard direct pressure on the wound with the cleanest cloth available — do not peek repeatedly.
2. Elevate if practical; keep pressing.
3. Use a commercial tourniquet high and tight only for life-threatening limb bleeding that pressure cannot stop; note the time.
Call emergency services (112 in Europe) if reachable — uncontrolled bleeding is a life threat.
Do NOT loosen a tourniquet every few minutes "to check."

## Q: How do I clean and dress a cut when no clinic is available?

A: 1. Irrigate thoroughly with drinkable water under pressure; remove debris.
2. Dress with clean gauze; change daily.
3. Watch for spreading redness, heat, swelling, pus, red streaks, or fever — those need professional care urgently.
Training beats gear; take a first-aid course before you need it.
Do NOT pack dirt, ash, or unproven home pastes into a deep wound.

## Q: What should be in a home first-aid kit?

A: Layer it: boo-boo (bandages, blister plasters, antiseptic, tweezers, tape), wound (gauze, elastic bandages, gloves, irrigation syringe, triangular bandages), trauma if trained (pressure bandage, hemostatic gauze, commercial tourniquet, chest seal, space blankets), plus oral rehydration salts, thermometer, and your usual medicines rotated. ## Q: How do I make oral rehydration solution?

A: For adult or child diarrhea/vomiting when dehydration is the risk: mix 1 litre of safe water + 6 level teaspoons sugar + ½ level teaspoon salt; sip constantly. Continue feeding children. Seek emergency or clinical care if reachable for severe dehydration, bloody stool, or a floppy/lethargic child. Hand and water hygiene protect the rest of the household.

## Q: How do I remove a tick safely?

A: 1. Grip with fine tweezers at the skin; pull steady and straight — no twisting or burning.
2. Disinfect the site; note the date.
3. Watch weeks for expanding rash or flu-like symptoms and seek medical attention if they appear.
Check skin after walks in forest, brush, or tall grass.
Do NOT smother with oil, burn, or jerk the body so the mouthparts snap off deeper.

## Q: What is the priority order for medical preparedness?

A: Training beats gear: certified first-aid/CPR first, wilderness first aid if you go remote, then equipment. An untrained person with a full trauma kit is less capable than a trained one with a bandana. Practice under supervision; in real emergencies contact emergency services if at all possible.

## Q: What hygiene rules prevent foodborne illness off-grid?

A: Cook thoroughly, keep raw and cooked separate, and do not leave perishable leftovers at room temperature beyond about 2 hours. Pair that with handwashing and safe water. In every prolonged disruption, sanitation and food hygiene prevent more misery than bandages.

## Q: What belongs in the trauma layer of a kit?

A: If trained: pressure bandage ("Israeli" style), hemostatic gauze, commercial tourniquet (know current protocols), chest seal, space blankets, headlamp, and trauma shears. Buy training before buying gear you do not understand. Uncontrolled bleeding and hypothermia are the field killers to plan for.

Also asked as: Israeli bandage tourniquet · hemostatic gauze kit · chest seal first aid

## Q: How do I treat diarrhea in a child off-grid?

A: 1. Offer WHO-style ORS constantly: 1 L safe water + 6 tsp sugar + ½ tsp salt.
2. Continue feeding; watch urine output and alertness.
3. Seek emergency care urgently if the child is lethargic, has bloody stool, or cannot keep fluids down — children dehydrate fastest.
Do NOT give only plain water for prolonged diarrhea without sodium/glucose, and do not wait for "it to pass" if they are worsening.

The most likely medical crisis in a long disruption is running out of daily prescription medicine; discuss an emergency buffer with your doctor within the law.

## Q: How do I irrigate a wound properly?

A: Clean early: flush with copious drinkable water under pressure (an irrigation syringe helps). Remove visible debris, dress, and reassess daily. Antiseptic can help around the skin; the mechanical flush matters most. Spreading infection signs mean professional care — field care has limits.

## Q: What are field first-aid priorities?

A: Priorities: stop severe bleeding (direct pressure first), protect airway and breathing, prevent hypothermia in any casualty, and get professional help. Carry and know your kit. Educational note only — take a certified first-aid course; hands-on practice is what works under stress.

## Q: How should I organise emergency food storage?

A: Use three layers: a working pantry of 2–4 weeks of what you already eat (FIFO rotation), extended storage of 3–12 months of staples (rice, oats, pasta, beans, lentils, cans, fats, salt, sugar), and deep storage of dry goods packed for years. Store cool, dark, and dry. Aim roughly 8,400 kJ (2,000 kcal) per adult per day, more for cold or heavy labour.

Also asked as: three layer pantry · how much food to store · FIFO food rotation

## Q: How do I pack dry goods for long-term storage?

A: Seal dry goods in Mylar bags with oxygen absorbers inside food-grade buckets. Use about 300–500 cc of absorber per 4 L of dry goods. Keep moisture ~10% or less — no brown rice, nuts, or oily goods for decades (brown rice ~6–12 months). Heat-seal, label, and date. White rice, wheat berries, beans, oats, pasta, sugar, and salt can last 20–30 years this way.

Also asked as: Mylar bags oxygen absorbers · bucket rice storage · store beans for decades

## Q: Why is botulism a risk with home canning?

A: Never taste-test a suspect can — botulism toxin is odorless. Discard bulging, leaking, or badly rusted cans. Learn pressure canning for low-acid foods (meat, broth, vegetables); a boiling-water bath is only safe for high-acid foods. Follow tested recipes exactly — improvised canning is a main modern source of botulism.

## Q: Can I eat canned food past the best-before date?

A: Canned food is often still safe well past best-before dates if cans are intact — not bulging, rusted through, or leaking. Quality declines before safety does. When in doubt, throw it out; never taste a suspect can because of botulism risk.

## Q: How do I cook without grid electricity?

A: Options: butane/propane camp stove (ventilate — every indoor flame makes CO), outdoor rocket stove on twigs, wood stove/fireplace with seasoned wood, solar cooker (120–180 °C on sunny days), and thermal cooking (boil then finish in heavy insulation to cut fuel use roughly in half). Cast iron and stainless survive open flame; nonstick often fails.

## Q: Is a boiling-water bath safe for canning meat?

A: No. A boiling-water bath is only safe for high-acid foods (jams, pickles, tomatoes with added acid). Meat, broth, and most vegetables need a pressure canner and tested times. Improvised shortcuts risk botulism — follow tested recipes exactly.

## Q: What are the ethics of hunting for food?

A: Hunt only where licensed and legal — seasons, species, methods, and tagging rules vary. Take hunter education, learn from mentors, and make clean vital-zone shots; if not confident, do not shoot. Ethical hunting means no waste and respect for the animal. Firearms safety rules are absolute.

## Q: How do I field dress a deer-sized animal?

A: 1. Move to a clean spot, on its back, head uphill; dress promptly (within the hour; faster when warm).
2. Ring-cut and tie off the anus; open the belly with blade facing up, fingers guarding — shallow cuts only.
3. Free the diaphragm, cut windpipe/esophagus high, roll organs out; prop the cavity open and cool fast.
Legal tagging before moving the animal.
Do NOT puncture gut or bladder — that taints meat.

## Q: How cold should game meat stay?

A: Keep raw meat below 4 °C except during active work. Never leave it for hours in the 4–60 °C danger zone. Cool the carcass as fast as possible after gutting — that decides quality more than fancy cuts. Cook ground wild meat through; wild boar and bear need thorough cooking (trichinella risk).

## Q: How do I field dress a rabbit safely?

A: Case-skin (hide peels like a glove), gut, and inspect the liver — spotted livers mean discard (tularemia risk). Always wear gloves handling rabbits. Birds: pluck or skin, remove crop and innards. Fish: bleed immediately, gut early, keep cold, and eat/salt/smoke/freeze the same day — fish spoil fastest.

## Q: How can I preserve meat without a freezer?

A: Options: jerky (lean strips dried with airflow; modern-safe practice includes heating to 71 °C), salt curing from tested recipes, hot smoking (cooks/flavours) vs cold smoking only with proper curing, pressure canning cubed meat/stock, and rendering fat to tallow. Curing is chemistry — use tested ratios, not improvisation.

## Q: Should I eat an animal I found dead?

A: Do not eat animals found dead, meat that smelled wrong at gutting, or carcasses with abscesses/lesions. Use carcass disease testing where offered (e.g. CWD regions). When regulations and common sense say no, the freezer stays empty — food poisoning or worse is not a calorie win.

## Q: What shot placement is ethical for deer?

## Q: How quickly should I field dress in warm weather?

## Q: Why wear gloves when handling rabbits?

## Q: How do I size backup power for my home?

A: Two numbers decide everything: peak watts (biggest simultaneous load) and watt-hours per day (watts × hours). Phones are tiny; fridges are ~1–1.5 kWh/day but spike 600–1200 W at start; kettles are 2000+ W. Match gear to needs — do not buy a gadget first and invent uses later.

## Q: What can a small power station run in an outage?

A: A 200–300 Wh bank covers phones, lights, and a router for a day. Around 1 kWh adds laptop and CPAP for a couple of days. Around 2 kWh can keep a modern fridge cycling roughly a day per charge — so solar input matters more than raw capacity beyond 48 hours.

Aim within ~30° of the sun, re-aim often, avoid even light shade, and keep panels cool and clean. A 400 W array + 2 kWh station is a realistic summer "fridge + comms" setup.

## Q: How should I run a petrol generator safely?

A: 1. Run outdoors only, 5+ m from windows, exhaust away from the house.
2. Use proper transfer gear installed by a qualified electrician — never backfeed through a wall outlet.
3. Run fridge/charging in 1–2 hour shifts a few times daily to cut fuel use 70–80%.
A typical 2 kW inverter unit burns roughly 0.5–1 L/h.
Do NOT run a generator in a garage, basement, or doorway "just for a minute."

## Q: Why is backfeeding a generator dangerous?

A: Backfeeding a generator into house wiring through a dryer outlet or "suicide cord" can energise lines and kill utility workers, and can destroy your generator when grid power returns. Only use a proper transfer switch or interlock installed by a qualified electrician.

## Q: What fridge start-up wattage surprises people?

## Q: How can generator shifts save fuel?

A: Instead of running a generator all day, run 1–2 hour shifts a few times daily to chill the fridge and charge battery stations. That pattern can cut fuel use by about 70–80% while keeping food safe. Still keep the unit outdoors and CO-safe every time.

## Q: What is a layered grid-down communications plan?

## Q: What do I do if someone is choking?

A: 1. Ask "Are you choking?" — if they can cough or speak, encourage coughing; do not slap the back yet.
2. If they cannot breathe, cough, or speak: give up to 5 sharp back blows between the shoulder blades, then up to 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich) for adults/children; repeat cycles.
3. Call emergency services (112 in Europe) if the obstruction does not clear or the person becomes unresponsive — start CPR if trained.
Educational note only: take a certified first-aid/CPR course. Do NOT do blind finger sweeps, and do not use abdominal thrusts on infants (use infant back blows / chest thrusts as trained).

Also asked as: choking first aid · someone choking what to do · heimlich maneuver basics · airway obstruction first aid · how do I help a choking person

## Q: How do I do CPR?

A: 1. Check responsiveness and breathing; shout for help and call emergency services (112 in Europe) if available.
2. If not breathing normally: hard, fast chest compressions in the centre of the chest (~100–120/min, depth about 5–6 cm on adults) — hands-only CPR is acceptable if untrained for breaths.
3. Continue until help takes over or the person shows clear signs of life.
Take a certified CPR course — this is educational, not a substitute for training. Do NOT delay compressions to hunt for a pulse for long.

## Q: What do I do for a snake bite?

A: 1. Keep the person calm and still; immobilise the bitten limb at heart level if practical.
2. Remove rings/watches before swelling; note the time.
3. Get emergency care urgently — antivenom decisions need clinicians.
Do NOT cut the wound, suck venom, apply ice, or use a tourniquet as "snakebite treatment." Photo the snake only if safe and distant.

## Q: How do I treat a broken bone in the field?

2. Check fingers/toes for colour, warmth, and sensation after splinting.
3. Treat for shock and get professional care; open fractures (bone through skin) need urgent evacuation.
Educational first aid only. Do NOT test the break by moving it repeatedly.

## Q: How do I purify air in a smoke emergency?

A: 1. Get indoors, close windows/doors, and shut off whole-house outdoor air intakes if you can.
2. Create a cleaner room: run a HEPA air purifier if available, or improvise a box-fan filter (fan sealed to a MERV-13 / HVAC filter) for particle reduction — not a gas mask substitute.
3. Avoid candles, frying, vacuuming, and smoking indoors; keep pets/kids in the cleanest room.
4. If outdoor air is clearer later, briefly ventilate; if anyone has chest pain, severe shortness of breath, or confusion, seek emergency care.
N95/FFP2 outdoors helps particles when you must go out; they do not remove all gases. Educational preparedness only.

## Q: How do I stay cool in a heatwave?

A: 1. Stay out of peak sun; use the coolest room, close blinds on sun-facing windows, and open windows only when outdoor air is cooler.
2. Drink water regularly; avoid heavy outdoor work midday.
3. Cool the body: damp cloths, cool showers, fans aimed at wet skin (not sealed rooms with only heat).
4. Check on elders, infants, and people without AC.
Seek emergency care for confusion, fainting, very high body temperature, or heat-stroke signs. Educational preparedness only.

## Q: How do I stop a nosebleed?

A: 1. Sit upright and lean slightly forward (not back — that sends blood down the throat).
2. Pinch the soft part of the nose firmly for 10–15 minutes without peeking.
3. Breathe through the mouth; spit blood rather than swallow lots of it.
Seek care if bleeding lasts beyond ~20–30 minutes, follows a head injury, or the person feels faint/dizzy. Do not pack random objects deep into the nose.

## Q: How do I find north using the stars?

A: Find the Big Dipper; the two pointer stars at the bowl’s end aim at Polaris, the North Star, which sits due north. Polaris’s height above the horizon roughly matches your latitude. Use stars to check compass work, not as an excuse to leave the map at home.

## Q: How do I use cordage in camp craft?

A: Cordage builds shelters, hangs pots, repairs gear, and lashes poles. Prefer learning a few strong knots over collecting fifty you forget. A toggle — a simple carved stick — makes quick-release connectors for ridgelines and loads.

## Q: How do I wash dishes without running water?

A: Use the three-basin method: (1) hot soapy wash, (2) hot rinse, (3) sanitizing rinse with a capful of bleach per few litres, then air dry. This keeps kitchen disease down when the grid and water are unreliable. Keep raw and cooked food tools separate.

## Q: How do I protect pipes from freezing in a prolonged cold blackout?

A: If freezing will be prolonged: drip taps or shut off and drain water lines. Pour a cup of salt water or plumbing-safe antifreeze into toilet bowls and traps. Keeping one warm room helps, but drained lines are the reliable protection for empty or unheated spaces.

## Q: How deep and far should I bury human waste?

A: When authorities allow field disposal: bury feces about 20+ cm deep and 60+ m from any water source, well away from camp. Prefer sealed bag storage for later proper disposal in populated emergencies. Never dump waste near streams you drink from.

## Q: How do I keep food waste from attracting animals?

A: Food waste draws animals fast. Seal it, burn burnables where legal and safe, or bury scraps appropriately away from shelter and water. In bear country follow local hang/canister rules. Dirty camps create dangerous wildlife problems.

Also asked as: bear attractant trash · bury food scraps camp · seal garbage emergency

## Q: How should I season firewood for a wood stove?

A: Store split wood dry under cover. Softwood often needs about 1 year of seasoning; hardwood 1–2 years. Wet wood wastes heat and clogs chimneys with creosote. A wood stove is the gold-standard safe household heat when installed and operated correctly.

## Q: How do I care for hollow-fiber filters day to day?

A: Follow the maker’s cleaning/backflush guidance, keep the dirty and clean sides from swapping, and never let the filter freeze. Carry chemical tablets or plan to boil so one clogged or damaged filter is not your only option.

## Q: Why pack space blankets in a trauma kit?

A: Hypothermia kills trauma patients even in mild weather once they are shocked and wet with blood or rain. After bleeding control and airway/breathing priorities, keep the casualty dry and insulated. Space blankets are light insurance beside gauze and gloves.

## Q: How do I care for a sprain without a clinic?

A: Rest, cool/ice, compression, and elevation. Buddy-tape injured fingers/toes to a neighbour. Improvised splints should pad the limb in the position found. If deformity, numbness, open fracture, or inability to bear weight after serious trauma — immobilise and seek emergency care when reachable.

## Q: Should sugar and honey get oxygen absorbers?

A: Sugar, salt, and honey need no oxygen absorber — absorbers can turn sugar into a brick. Honey keeps essentially forever; hardened honey re-liquefies in warm water. Still store them dry, labelled, and rodent-proof.

## Q: How long does a butane canister last for cooking?

A: A 220 g butane canister gives roughly 1.5–2 hours of moderate flame. A 9 kg propane cylinder (~18 L liquid) runs a two-burner camp stove for tens of hours. Always ventilate; never use camp stoves as indoor heaters. Check cylinder fill levels rather than assuming exchange tanks are full.

Also asked as: 220g butane burn time · 9 kg propane how long · camp stove fuel planning

## Q: What fats are best for long-term food storage?

A: Ghee and tallow are outstanding preparedness fats — shelf-stable for years, high smoke point, and about 9 kcal/g. Regular oils go rancid faster. Pair fats with staples; salt is critical and cheap — store several kilograms.

Also asked as: ghee tallow preparedness · shelf stable cooking fat · store cooking oil emergency

## Q: How do I get vitamin C from stored dry foods?

A: Sprouting jars turn stored lentils and seeds into fresh greens with vitamin C in 3–5 days — one of the few renewable "fresh" foods in a long outage. A small garden of potatoes, cabbage, kale, and roots also extends the pantry. Forage only species you know with certainty.

Also asked as: sprouting lentils emergency · fresh greens grid down · scurvy prevention storage

## Q: What is appetite fatigue in emergencies?

A: Store what you eat and eat what you store. Appetite fatigue is real, especially in children — familiar foods, spices, coffee/tea, honey, and some chocolate support morale and actual calorie intake. Rotation cooking from deep storage keeps both skill and appetite current.

Also asked as: store what you eat · comfort food preparedness · kids won't eat storage food

## Q: What garden crops help most for preparedness?

A: Favour calorie-dense, climate-suited crops: potatoes, cabbage, kale, and root vegetables, then preserve the harvest. Grow what thrives where you live. A modest garden plus a rotated pantry beats a huge unused seed vault with no practice.

Also asked as: calorie dense garden survival · cold hardy emergency crops · grow food for pantry

## Q: Why is salt critical in food storage?

A: Salt is critical for cooking, food preservation, and electrolytes, and it is nearly free to store in depth. Keep several kilograms in a dry place. It also supports morale because bland storage food becomes edible with seasoning.

## Q: How does thermal cooking save fuel?

A: Bring food to a boil on your stove, then seal the pot in a heavily insulated box or thermal cooker so residual heat finishes rice, beans, and stews. This can roughly halve fuel use — decisive when propane or wood is limited.

## Q: How do I store oxygen absorbers correctly when packing food?

A: Use about 300–500 cc of oxygen absorber capacity per 4 L of packed dry goods; err high. Work quickly once absorbers are exposed to air, seal Mylar promptly, and confirm the bag "bricks" within a day or two. Do not use absorbers with sugar if you want loose crystals.

## Q: What comfort items belong in family food plans?

A: For children: familiar snacks and warm clothes that fit. For adults: coffee/tea, honey, or chocolate in the pantry plan. Morale is a survival tool — stressed people make worse decisions and stop eating unfamiliar food.

Also asked as: kids bug out comfort · morale emergency kit · chocolate coffee prep

## Q: How do I hang and age venison safely?

A: After rigor (~24 h), hang at 0–6 °C for about 2–7 days (younger animals shorter). Above ~7 °C, skip aging and process immediately. Skin while fresh and hung; clean under the pelvic bone early — left organs spoil first. Package tightly, labelled, and freeze at −18 °C for a year or more when well wrapped.

Also asked as: age deer carcass · hang meat temperature · skinning hanging deer

## Q: What cuts come from a deer carcass?

A: Tenderloins (inside under the spine — remove early), backstraps along the outside of the spine, hindquarter roasts along natural seams, front shoulders for stew/grind/slow cook, and neck/flank/trim for grind with 10–20% added fat. Trim hair, silverskin, and blood-shot tissue; keep meat cold while working.

Also asked as: deer butchering map · backstrap tenderloin ham · break down venison

## Q: What belongs in a hunting kill kit?

A: Sharp fixed-blade knife plus spare/sharpener, nitrile gloves, strong cord or zip ties, game bags, optional bone saw, paper towels, headlamp, licenses/tags, and a plan to cool meat fast in warm weather. Scouting, wind awareness, and marksmanship matter more than gadgets.

Also asked as: hunt field kit list · game bags gloves knife · what to carry hunting

## Q: How do I scout before a hunt?

A: Learn tracks, droppings, trails, feeding sign, and bedding areas. Hunt into the wind — deer smell you first. Patience and practiced marksmanship at real distances fill freezers more than expensive camouflage. Know and follow local law.

Also asked as: hunt scouting sign · deer tracks trails wind · before the hunt skills

## Q: How do I make jerky more safely?

A: Slice lean meat about 5 mm thick, salt/brine or marinade, and dry with airflow until strips bend and crack but do not snap. Modern safer practice includes heating to 71 °C. Store dry and watch for mould; when unsure, discard.

Also asked as: safe jerky temperature · dry meat strips · jerky without fridge

## Q: What temperature is unsafe for aging meat?

A: Hang/age around 0–6 °C. Above about 7 °C, bacteria beat helpful enzymes — skip aging, butcher promptly, and get meat cold. A cold garage in the right season or a fridge for quarters beats a warm shed "for a few days."

Also asked as: aging meat too warm · 7°C hang limit · skip aging process immediately

## Q: How do I package venison for the freezer?

A: Work clean and cold. Wrap tightly in butcher paper, vacuum bags, or heavy freezer bags with air pressed out; label cut and date. At −18 °C, well-wrapped venison keeps a year or more. Poor wrapping causes freezer burn more than "mystery spoilage."

## Q: What is hot smoking versus cold smoking for meat?

A: Hot-smoking cooks and flavours meat — eat or refrigerate afterward. Cold-smoking preserves only when combined with proper curing; smoke alone is not a safety method. Learn tested processes rather than improvising "old ways" that miss critical salt or time steps.

## Q: How long does petrol store for generators?

A: Petrol stores about 6–12 months (longer with stabilizer). Diesel and propane store for years — dual-fuel propane generators pair well with the same 9 kg cylinders as your stove. Rotate fuel; test-run the generator on a schedule.

## Q: What is a UPS useful for in blackouts?

A: A line-interactive UPS with AVR protects routers, computers, and NAS through sags, spikes, and short outages so you can shut down cleanly. Runtime claims assume full load — at ~50 W of network gear, even a small unit may last 30–60 minutes. Pair with surge protection at the panel and point-of-use strips.

## Q: Why choose LiFePO4 batteries for solar storage?

A: For permanent solar, an MPPT charge controller, LiFePO4 bank, and proper fusing are the modern standard. LiFePO4 offers 3,000+ cycles versus roughly 500 for lead-acid and tolerates partial charging. Mains wiring and transfer switches still need a qualified electrician.

## Q: How do I aim portable solar panels?

A: Point panels within about 30° of the sun and re-aim every couple of hours. Even light shade on one corner can halve output. Keep surfaces clean and cool. Winter at high latitude needs far more panel area or alternate charging.

## Q: What should go in a Faraday bin?

Insulate each item from the metal enclosure, label the bin and check the inventory yearly. Test your day-to-day gear often; sealed spares are insurance, not your only radio.

## Q: What is a realistic summer solar fridge setup?

A: A roughly 400 W panel array plus a ~2 kWh station is a realistic summer setup to keep a fridge and communications running indefinitely if you manage loads and sun. Winter at high latitude needs far more panel or generator support. Do the watt-hour math for your own fridge.

## Q: How much does a typical fridge use per day?

A: A modern fridge often uses roughly 1–1.5 kWh per day because it cycles. That is why a ~2 kWh station is a common "about one day of fridge" rule of thumb before solar or generator recharging. Keep the door closed and pre-chill hard before an expected outage.

## Q: How do propane dual-fuel generators help logistics?

## Q: Why get an amateur radio licence for preparedness?

A: A licence unlocks VHF/UHF handhelds through local repeaters (tens of km) and HF that can reach across countries with no commercial infrastructure. Repeaters may have backup power; simplex works when they do not. The exam is modest and the community trains newcomers — often the biggest single jump in comms capability.

Also asked as: HAM license emergency · why learn amateur radio · VHF UHF HF prep

## Q: Do I need a licence to listen to amateur radio?

A: Listening is legal almost everywhere. Transmitting on amateur (HAM) bands requires a licence in nearly all countries; licence-free bands have strict power limits. Start with a good receive radio, then study for a licence if you want to transmit.

## Q: How should phones be used when networks are congested?

A: Send short texts rather than calls when networks are stressed — SMS often gets through when voice cannot. Immediately enable low-power mode, reduce brightness, and kill background data. Preserve battery for check-ins on your written schedule rather than endless scrolling.

## Q: What check-in schedule works for family radios?

## Q: What is the role of shortwave in emergencies?

## Q: Why keep duplicate chargers and antennas?

## Q: How do I harden home security without gadgets first?

## Q: What are tornado warning signs?

A: Learn your region’s hazards. Tornado clues can include a greenish sky, sudden calm after strong storms, large hail, a persistent rotating wall cloud, and a continuous freight-train roar. When in doubt, take shelter in the lowest interior room away from windows. Follow official local guidance when broadcasts are available.

## Q: How much should a go bag weigh?

## Q: What is SODIS water treatment?

A: SODIS uses a clear PET bottle filled with clear water left in full sun for about 6 hours (UV kills many microbes). UV pens work similarly but need batteries. Only use clear water — cloudy water blocks UV. Boiling or a good filter-plus-chemical stack is more reliable when fuel or tablets are available.

## Q: How do I take a bath with little water?

## Q: What laundry method works without a machine?
