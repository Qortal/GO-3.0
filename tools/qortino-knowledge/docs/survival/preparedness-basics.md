# Household Preparedness Basics

*Educational preparedness information for emergencies. Follow local laws and
official guidance during real events.*

## Priorities in order

A useful ordering for any emergency: shelter and warmth, water, food, light
and power, communication, security, sanitation, and morale. Most household
emergencies are mundane — storms, blackouts, supply disruptions — so prepare
for boring problems first and exotic ones second.

## Water

Store a minimum of 4 litres per person per day; two weeks is a solid
household baseline. Rotate stored water yearly. Have at least two ways to
make questionable water safe: boiling (a rolling boil for 1 minute; 3 minutes
above ~2,000 m elevation), a quality filter, and unscented household bleach
as backup (about 2 drops per litre of clear water, wait 30 minutes).

## Food storage

Build depth in food you already eat: rice, oats, beans, lentils, pasta,
canned meat/fish/vegetables, salt, sugar, fats, coffee/tea. Store cool, dark,
and dry; first-in-first-out rotation. Dry goods sealed in Mylar bags with
oxygen absorbers inside buckets keep for years — note that sharp pasta can
puncture vacuum bags, so pad or double-bag it. A pressure canner extends
storage to meats and low-acid foods.

## Backup power

Size backup power by asking two questions: what is the peak wattage of the
devices you must run, and how many watt-hours per day do they consume? A
small power station covers phones, lights, a router, and a CPAP; a ~2 kWh
unit can keep a fridge cycling. A UPS (ideally line-interactive with
automatic voltage regulation) protects routers, computers, and NAS boxes
through short outages and dirty power. Fuel-based generators need
ventilation, fuel rotation, and carbon-monoxide awareness — never run one
indoors.

## Heat, light, cooking

Blackout basics: headlamps beat candles for safety, wool blankets and
sleeping bags concentrate warmth in one room, and a camp stove or propane
burner (used with ventilation) covers cooking. A standard 9 kg propane
cylinder holds roughly 18 litres of fuel — know how many you have and check
fill levels rather than assuming exchange tanks are full.

## Home security fundamentals

Fix the weak points before buying gadgets: solid doors with long screws in
strike plates and hinges, locked windows, trimmed shrubs that remove hiding
spots, exterior lighting, and lived-in cues when away. Cameras and alarms are
layers on top of hardened basics, not substitutes.

## Documents, cash, comms

Keep copies of key documents (ID, deeds, insurance, prescriptions) in a
waterproof folder and encrypted digital backup. Hold some cash in small
notes — card networks fail in blackouts. A battery/hand-crank radio covers
official broadcasts; agree on an out-of-area contact person with family in
case local networks jam.

## Severe weather awareness

Learn your region's specific hazards and the warning signs. For example,
tornado clues include a greenish sky, sudden calm after strong storms, large
hail, a persistent rotating wall cloud, and a continuous freight-train roar —
when in doubt, take shelter in the lowest interior room away from windows.

## The 72-hour bag (bug-out / go bag)

One per person, packed and by the door — for evacuation (fire, flood, gas
leak), not adventure. Core contents: water (1-2 L carried + filter or
purifier bottle + chlorine dioxide tablets), calorie-dense no-cook food
(nuts, bars, tinned fish — ~8,000 kJ/day), full clothing change with wool
socks and rain shell, sturdy shoes stored with the bag, shelter (emergency
bivvy or blanket + compact tarp), fire (lighter + ferro rod + tinder),
knife or multitool, headlamp + spare lithium batteries, first-aid kit with
personal medication for a week, hygiene kit, powerbank + cables,
hand-crank/battery radio, cash in small notes, copies of documents in a
waterproof pouch, paper map with rally points marked, whistle, work gloves,
duct tape, heavy contractor bags (shelter, poncho, water carry — a dozen
uses), and comfort items for children. Total target under 10-12 kg — a bag
you can actually walk with. Review contents every 6 months (clothing sizes,
medication dates, batteries).

## Vehicle kit

Blanket or sleeping bag, water, snacks, first-aid kit, headlamp, jump pack
or cables, tow strap, basic tools, work gloves, reflective vest and warning
triangle, phone charger, paper map, seasonal additions (ice scraper,
traction aid, folding shovel in winter). Keep the tank above half — fuel
stations fail with the grid.

## Community, cash, and barter

Lone-wolf survival is a myth: know your neighbors before you need them —
skills nearby (medical, mechanical, electrical), who has generators or
wood stoves, who is vulnerable (elderly, disabled) and will need checking
on. Hold two to four weeks of expenses in cash at home in small
denominations — card terminals, ATMs, and online banking all ride on the
grid. Practical barter depth beyond your own needs: extra fuel canisters,
batteries, lighters, water treatment, soap, salt, coffee, and skills —
these trade better than gold in short crises. Guard operational security:
casual talk about your supplies invites problems; the best preparation
looks boring from the street.

## Practice runs

Preps that were never tested fail in creative ways. Run a "grid-down
weekend": main breaker off Friday evening, live on your systems until
Sunday. You will discover which lantern eats batteries, that someone's
medication is at half, and that the camp stove's regulator is missing — on
a weekend when it's funny rather than fatal. Cook from deep storage
occasionally; rotate and re-date as you go.
